[Q1] What does cosine similarity measure, and when does it equal the dot product?	The angle between two vectors, ignoring their length. It equals the dot product when both vectors are L2-normalised.	bank level::basic
[Q2] What is the SVD and why does it matter for ML?	Any matrix factors as UΣVᵀ; keeping the top k singular values gives the best rank-k approximation. It underlies PCA, matrix factorisation and the low-rank idea behind LoRA.	bank level::basic
[Q3] State Bayes' rule and give an ML use.	P(A|B) = P(B|A)P(A)/P(B). Naive Bayes classifiers; turning a test's sensitivity and base rate into the probability of fraud given a flag.	bank level::basic
[Q4] MLE vs MAP?	MLE picks parameters maximising the likelihood of the data; MAP adds a prior and maximises the posterior. L2 regularisation is MAP with a Gaussian prior; L1 with a Laplace prior.	bank level::intermediate
[Q5] Why is cross-entropy the loss for classification?	It is the negative log-likelihood of the labels under the model's predicted distribution, so minimising it is maximum likelihood; it also gives well-behaved gradients (p − y) with softmax or sigmoid.	bank level::intermediate
[Q6] What is KL divergence and where does it appear in LLM work?	A non-symmetric measure of how one distribution differs from another. It appears in RLHF (penalty keeping the policy close to the reference model), distillation (matching teacher distributions), and variational methods.	bank level::intermediate
[Q7] Explain the chain rule's role in training neural networks.	Backpropagation applies the chain rule to compute the gradient of the loss with respect to every parameter, layer by layer from the output back.	bank level::basic
[Q8] What is entropy, intuitively?	The average surprise of a distribution: high when outcomes are uncertain, zero when one outcome is certain. Decision trees use it to measure impurity.	bank level::intermediate
[Q9] Define a 95% confidence interval correctly.	A procedure that, over repeated samples, produces intervals containing the true value 95% of the time; not a 95% probability for this specific interval.	bank level::basic ch::2
[Q10] What is the difference between type I and type II errors?	Type I: a false positive (claiming an effect that is not there), probability α. Type II: a false negative, probability β; power is 1 − β.	bank level::basic ch::2
[Q11] How does sample size depend on the minimum detectable effect?	Inversely with its square: halving the MDE needs four times the sample.	bank level::basic ch::2
[Q12] What is CUPED?	Variance reduction using a pre-experiment covariate: Y − θ(X − mean X) with θ = cov(Y,X)/var(X); variance falls by 1 − ρ² without bias.	bank level::intermediate ch::2
[Q13] Why is peeking at A/B results a problem?	Repeated significance checks inflate the false positive rate far above α; use a fixed horizon or a sequential method built for interim looks.	bank level::intermediate ch::2
[Q14] What is sample ratio mismatch and what do you do?	The observed traffic split differs significantly from the design; stop interpreting results and find the assignment or logging bug.	bank level::intermediate ch::2
[Q15] Bonferroni vs Benjamini-Hochberg?	Bonferroni controls the chance of any false positive (strict); BH controls the expected share of false discoveries (more power), better for many metrics or segments.	bank level::intermediate ch::2
[Q16] How do you test a change to courier dispatch?	Not by user randomisation, because arms share couriers. Switchbacks over city × time slots, or geo experiments, analysed at the slot level with clustered errors and carryover buffers.	bank level::senior ch::2
[Q17] An A/B test shows +4% but leadership doubts it. What do you check?	SRM, data quality, novelty over time, segment consistency, power and the winner's curse, interference, guardrails, and whether the effect replicates in a holdout or follow-up test.	bank level::senior ch::2
[Q18] What assumption does difference-in-differences rely on?	Parallel trends: without treatment, treated and control groups would have changed alike; check pre-period trends.	bank level::intermediate ch::2
[Q19] Bias vs variance?	Bias: error from too simple a model (underfitting). Variance: error from sensitivity to the training sample (overfitting). Regularisation, data and model choice trade them.	bank level::basic ch::3
[Q20] Why can't you use the test set to choose hyperparameters?	It then becomes part of training decisions and no longer gives an unbiased estimate.	bank level::basic ch::3
[Q21] L1 or L2 regularisation for feature selection?	L1, because it drives some weights exactly to zero.	bank level::basic ch::3
[Q22] Name four types of leakage.	Target-derived features, future information in time-based features, preprocessing fitted on all data, and the same entity across train and test.	bank level::intermediate ch::3
[Q23] How would you cross-validate a model predicting next week's demand?	Walk-forward (time-based) folds with a gap equal to the horizon and point-in-time features.	bank level::intermediate ch::3
[Q24] How does Adam differ from SGD with momentum?	Adam adds per-parameter adaptive step sizes from a running average of squared gradients, with bias correction.	bank level::intermediate ch::3
[Q25] How do you handle a categorical feature with 50,000 values?	Out-of-fold smoothed target encoding, hashing, frequency encoding, or learned embeddings in a neural network; CatBoost handles it natively.	bank level::intermediate ch::3
[Q26] What do you do first with heavy class imbalance?	Choose the right metric (PR-AUC, recall at a precision), then class weights and threshold tuning; resample only training folds; correct probabilities afterwards.	bank level::intermediate ch::3
[Q27] Offline AUC 0.99. Your reaction?	Suspect leakage: inspect top features, timestamps, splits and duplicates; compare with simple baselines.	bank level::senior ch::3
[Q28] When should a problem not be solved with ML?	When rules suffice, labels or feedback do not exist, errors are unacceptable and unexplainable, or nobody will act on predictions.	bank level::senior ch::3
[Q29] How does a decision tree choose a split?	It tries features and thresholds and picks the split with the largest reduction in impurity (Gini, entropy or variance).	bank level::basic ch::40
[Q30] Random forest vs gradient boosting?	Forests average deep, decorrelated trees in parallel (variance reduction); boosting adds shallow trees sequentially fitting the gradient (bias reduction), usually more accurate when tuned.	bank level::basic ch::40
[Q31] Why does a small learning rate help gradient boosting?	Shrinkage regularises: each tree corrects a little, and with early stopping the ensemble generalises better at the cost of more trees.	bank level::intermediate ch::40
[Q32] Which models need feature scaling?	Linear and logistic regression with regularisation, SVMs, kNN, k-means, PCA and neural networks; trees do not.	bank level::intermediate ch::40
[Q33] What is the kernel trick?	Computing dot products in a high-dimensional feature space via a kernel function, so linear methods learn non-linear boundaries.	bank level::intermediate ch::40
[Q34] How do you choose k in k-means?	Elbow, silhouette, stability, and usefulness for the task; with k-means++ initialisation and restarts.	bank level::intermediate ch::40
[Q35] When would you choose logistic regression over LightGBM in production?	Interpretability or regulation, very small data, critical calibration, extreme latency limits, or when the accuracy gain does not justify complexity.	bank level::senior ch::40
[Q36] Why do trees still beat deep learning on most tabular data?	They handle heterogeneous, irregular features and uninformative features well, need little tuning, and deep nets' smoothness bias fits tabular targets poorly; deep models win with huge data, sequences or high-cardinality IDs.	bank level::senior ch::40
[Q37] Precision vs recall?	Precision: share of flagged that are positive. Recall: share of positives that are flagged.	bank level::basic ch::4
[Q38] What does ROC-AUC mean?	The probability a random positive scores above a random negative.	bank level::basic ch::4
[Q39] Why prefer PR-AUC for rare positives?	ROC-AUC stays high when false positives are many but small relative to the huge negative class; PR-AUC exposes poor precision.	bank level::intermediate ch::4
[Q40] What is calibration and how do you fix it?	Predicted probabilities matching observed rates; fix with Platt scaling, isotonic regression or temperature scaling on held-out data.	bank level::intermediate ch::4
[Q41] MAE, RMSE or WAPE for demand forecasting?	WAPE for aggregate accuracy across items of different volumes; MAE for interpretability; RMSE when large misses are costly; always report bias.	bank level::intermediate ch::4
[Q42] Micro vs macro F1?	Micro pools all decisions (dominated by frequent classes); macro averages per-class F1 (rare classes count equally).	bank level::intermediate ch::4
[Q43] How do you compare two models' AUCs fairly?	Paired bootstrap over the independent unit (users, queries) on the same test set, or DeLong's test; report the interval of the difference.	bank level::senior ch::4
[Q44] Offline metric up, online flat: list causes.	Proxy mismatch, different candidate sets, leakage, train-serve skew, feedback-loop bias in logs, low power, or opposing segment effects.	bank level::senior ch::4
[Q45] Why do neural networks need non-linear activations?	Without them, stacked layers collapse into one linear function.	bank level::basic ch::5_6
[Q46] Why ReLU (or GELU) instead of sigmoid in hidden layers?	Sigmoid saturates, making gradients vanish in deep networks; ReLU-family activations keep gradients alive and are cheap.	bank level::basic ch::5_6
[Q47] Why does initialisation matter? Name a scheme.	Bad scales make activations and gradients explode or vanish through layers. Xavier/Glorot for tanh, He/Kaiming for ReLU keep variance roughly constant.	bank level::intermediate ch::5_6
[Q48] Batch norm vs layer norm?	Batch norm normalises each feature across the batch (CNNs, stable large batches); layer norm normalises across features per example (transformers, variable lengths, small batches).	bank level::intermediate ch::5_6
[Q49] What does dropout do?	Randomly zeroes activations in training, preventing co-adaptation; acts like an ensemble; disabled at inference (with scaling handled in training).	bank level::intermediate ch::5_6
[Q50] Your loss is NaN after a few steps. What do you check?	Learning rate too high, missing gradient clipping, log of zero or division by zero, fp16 overflow (use bf16 or loss scaling), bad input data.	bank level::intermediate ch::5_6
[Q51] The model does not learn at all. How do you debug?	Overfit a single batch first; check labels and inputs line up; verify loss and output shapes; check the learning rate; confirm optimizer.zero_grad(), model.train() and that gradients flow (no detached tensors).	bank level::intermediate ch::5_6
[Q52] What does	model.eval() change? Disables dropout and makes batch norm use running statistics; it does not turn off gradients (use torch.no_grad() for that).	bank level::basic ch::5_6
[Q53] What is mixed-precision training?	Computing in bf16 or fp16 while keeping critical values (master weights, reductions) in fp32; faster and smaller with near-identical results.	bank level::intermediate ch::5_6
[Q54] What is a learning-rate warm-up and why use it?	Start with a small learning rate and increase it over the first steps, avoiding unstable early updates when Adam's statistics are poor; standard for transformers.	bank level::intermediate ch::5_6
[Q55] How does word2vec learn embeddings?	Predicting context words from a word (skip-gram) or the reverse, with negative sampling; words in similar contexts get similar vectors.	bank level::basic ch::8_9
[Q56] Why did LSTMs replace vanilla RNNs, and why did transformers replace LSTMs?	LSTM gates ease vanishing gradients over long sequences; transformers removed sequential computation, allowing parallel training and direct long-range attention.	bank level::intermediate ch::8_9
[Q57] What is BPE tokenisation?	Start from characters or bytes and repeatedly merge the most frequent pair into a new token, giving a sub-word vocabulary that handles rare words.	bank level::basic ch::8_9
[Q58] Why do tokenisers matter for cost and multilingual quality?	Cost and context are counted in tokens; languages under-represented in the tokeniser's training (often Arabic, among others) need more tokens per word, so they are slower, costlier and often lower quality.	bank level::intermediate ch::8_9
[Q59] Write the attention formula.	softmax(QKᵀ/√dₖ + mask)V.	bank level::basic ch::10
[Q60] Why the √dₖ?	Keeps dot products from growing with dimension and saturating softmax.	bank level::basic ch::10
[Q61] What does each part of a transformer block do?	Attention mixes information across positions; the feed-forward network transforms each position; residuals and normalisation keep deep training stable.	bank level::intermediate ch::10
[Q62] What is RoPE?	Rotary position embeddings rotate queries and keys by position-dependent angles so attention depends on relative distance.	bank level::intermediate ch::10
[Q63] What is the KV cache?	Stored keys and values of previous tokens per layer so each new token only computes its own; memory grows with layers × KV heads × head dim × tokens.	bank level::intermediate ch::10
[Q64] MQA and GQA?	Query heads share key/value heads (all in MQA, in groups in GQA), shrinking the KV cache and speeding decoding.	bank level::intermediate ch::10
[Q65] Why is attention quadratic, and what are the main ways around it?	Every token attends to every other: O(T²). FlashAttention reduces memory traffic (still exact); sliding-window, sparse or linear attention and state-space models reduce the computation itself.	bank level::senior ch::10
[Q66] Encoder vs decoder models: which for what?	Encoders (BERT) for classification, extraction and embeddings; decoders (GPT) for generation and general LLM use.	bank level::basic ch::12_15
[Q67] What is a mixture-of-experts model?	Each token is routed to a few expert feed-forward networks, so total parameters are large but compute per token is small; needs load balancing.	bank level::intermediate ch::12_15
[Q68] What do scaling laws say?	Loss falls predictably as a power law in parameters, data and compute; Chinchilla showed compute-optimal training uses roughly 20 tokens per parameter, so many earlier models were under-trained.	bank level::intermediate ch::12_15
[Q69] What are the stages of training a chat LLM?	Pre-training on large text corpora (next-token prediction), supervised fine-tuning on instructions and conversations, then preference optimisation (RLHF or DPO), often followed by reinforcement learning for reasoning.	bank level::basic ch::12_15
[Q70] How does RLHF work?	Collect human preference pairs, train a reward model on them, then optimise the policy with RL (classically PPO) to maximise reward with a KL penalty to stay close to the SFT model.	bank level::intermediate ch::12_15
[Q71] What does DPO change?	It optimises the policy directly on preference pairs with a classification-style loss derived from the RLHF objective, with no separate reward model or RL loop.	bank level::intermediate ch::12_15
[Q72] What are reasoning models and how are they trained?	Models that generate long reasoning before answering, trained largely with reinforcement learning on tasks with verifiable rewards (maths, code tests), so they learn to search, check and correct; controlled at inference with a reasoning budget.	bank level::senior ch::12_15
[Q73] What is distillation?	Training a smaller student to imitate a larger teacher's outputs (labels, soft probabilities or generated text), keeping much of the quality at lower cost.	bank level::intermediate ch::12_15
[Q74] Small fine-tuned model or large general model for a production task?	Compare on your evaluation set with cost and latency: large models for broad, changing or low-volume tasks; small fine-tuned models for narrow, high-volume, latency-sensitive tasks, often distilled from the large one.	bank level::senior ch::12_15
[Q75] Temperature, top-k and top-p?	Temperature scales logits (lower is more deterministic); top-k samples from the k most likely tokens; top-p from the smallest set whose probability mass reaches p.	bank level::basic ch::21
[Q76] Greedy decoding vs beam search for chat models?	Beam search finds high-likelihood sequences but tends to be bland and repetitive for open-ended text; chat uses sampling; beam search suits translation-like tasks.	bank level::intermediate ch::21
[Q77] What is continuous batching?	Adding new requests to the running batch at each decoding step instead of waiting for the whole batch to finish, keeping GPUs busy and raising throughput (vLLM, TGI).	bank level::intermediate ch::21
[Q78] What is speculative decoding?	A small draft model proposes several tokens; the large model verifies them in one forward pass and accepts the matching prefix; same output distribution, lower latency.	bank level::intermediate ch::21
[Q79] What does quantisation trade off?	Lower precision (int8, int4: GPTQ, AWQ) cuts memory and raises speed, with some quality loss that grows at lower bits and for smaller models; measure on your tasks.	bank level::intermediate ch::21
[Q80] Why is decoding memory-bandwidth-bound?	Each step generates one token per sequence but must read all weights and the KV cache; batching amortises the reads.	bank level::intermediate ch::21
[Q81] What are time to first token and tokens per second, and what drives each?	TTFT is dominated by prefill (prompt length, queueing); tokens per second by decode speed (model size, batching, bandwidth, speculative decoding).	bank level::intermediate ch::21
[Q82] Estimate the monthly cost of an LLM feature.	Calls per month × (input tokens × input price + output tokens × output price), minus caching and batch discounts; compare with self-hosting (GPU hours at the needed throughput and utilisation).	bank level::senior ch::21
[Q83] Your offline LLM classification job is too expensive. Options?	Batch API (often around half price), prompt caching, shorter prompts, a smaller model or a cascade, and distilling into a small model; a before-and-after cost figure from your own work is the strongest answer.	bank level::senior ch::21
[Q84] When would you self-host an open model instead of using an API?	High, steady volume where GPUs stay busy; data residency or privacy requirements; need for fine-tuning or custom decoding; latency control. Against: operational cost, scaling, and keeping up with model releases.	bank level::senior ch::21
[Q85] Zero-shot vs few-shot?	Instructions only vs instructions plus examples; add examples when format or boundary cases are ambiguous.	bank level::basic ch::22
[Q86] How do you get reliable JSON?	Structured-output or tool-calling modes with a schema, validation with Pydantic, one retry with the error, and a fallback.	bank level::basic ch::22
[Q87] When does chain of thought help?	Multi-step reasoning; not simple extraction. Reasoning models do it internally under a budget.	bank level::intermediate ch::22
[Q88] How do you version and test prompts?	Version template, model and parameters together; run a labelled evaluation set on each change; log versions with outputs.	bank level::intermediate ch::22
[Q89] How do you design a prompt for caching?	Stable content first (system prompt, instructions, examples, tools), byte-identical across calls; per-request content last.	bank level::intermediate ch::22
[Q90] What is indirect prompt injection and how do you defend?	Instructions hidden in content the model reads (documents, emails, tool output). Least privilege, policies in code, human approval for risky actions, untrusted content handled by a tool-less reader, filtering and red-teaming.	bank level::senior ch::22
[Q91] Prompt, RAG or fine-tune?	Prompt first; RAG for knowledge that changes or must be cited; fine-tune for consistent format or style, narrow high-volume tasks, or domain language.	bank level::basic ch::20
[Q92] Explain LoRA in one sentence.	Freeze W and learn a low-rank update BA (scaled by α/r), training well under 1% of parameters and merging afterwards at no latency cost.	bank level::basic ch::20
[Q93] Why is B initialised to zero in LoRA?	So the update starts at zero and training begins exactly from the pretrained model.	bank level::intermediate ch::20
[Q94] What makes QLoRA possible?	4-bit NF4 quantisation of the frozen base, double quantisation and paged optimisers, with bf16 LoRA adapters receiving gradients.	bank level::intermediate ch::20
[Q95] How do you limit catastrophic forgetting?	PEFT, low learning rate, few epochs, replay of general data, and a regression suite.	bank level::intermediate ch::20
[Q96] How would you evaluate whether a fine-tune was worth it?	Against the best prompted baseline on a held-out set, plus format reliability, a general-ability and safety regression suite, and cost and latency; include maintenance cost when the base model changes.	bank level::senior ch::20
[Q97] How are sentence embeddings trained?	Contrastively: pairs that should match are pulled together and in-batch negatives pushed apart (InfoNCE).	bank level::basic ch::29
[Q98] What does the temperature do in InfoNCE?	Sharpens or softens the softmax over similarities; low temperature focuses on hard negatives.	bank level::intermediate ch::29
[Q99] What are false negatives in hard-negative mining?	Mined "negatives" that are actually relevant; filter them with a cross-encoder or margin, or skip top ranks.	bank level::intermediate ch::29
[Q100] What are Matryoshka embeddings?	Embeddings trained so that prefixes (first 64, 128, … dimensions) are good embeddings too, allowing cheap truncated search.	bank level::intermediate ch::29
[Q101] How do you choose an embedding model?	Shortlist from MTEB for your task and languages, evaluate on your own labelled queries, compare latency, dimension, cost and licence, consider fine-tuning.	bank level::senior ch::29
[Q102] BM25 in one sentence.	Keyword scoring summing IDF-weighted, saturating, length-normalised term frequencies.	bank level::basic ch::30
[Q103] Why hybrid search?	Keyword search catches exact names and codes; embeddings catch paraphrases; fused (RRF) they cover each other's misses.	bank level::basic ch::30
[Q104] How does HNSW work?	A layered proximity graph searched greedily from sparse top layers down to the full bottom layer; efSearch trades recall for latency.	bank level::intermediate ch::30
[Q105] What is product quantisation?	Splitting vectors into sub-vectors, each stored as the id of its nearest of 256 centroids; scored with lookup tables; compressed and approximate.	bank level::intermediate ch::30
[Q106] Bi-encoder vs cross-encoder?	Bi-encoders embed separately (fast, indexable, for retrieval); cross-encoders read query and document together (accurate, slow, for re-ranking).	bank level::intermediate ch::30
[Q107] How would you handle per-user permissions in vector search?	Filter in retrieval (pre-filtering or a partition per tenant), never by prompt instructions; test that restricted documents never reach the context.	bank level::senior ch::30
[Q108] Draw the RAG pipeline.	Offline: load, parse, chunk, embed and index. Online: rewrite query, hybrid retrieval with filters, re-rank, assemble context, generate with citations, verify.	bank level::basic ch::31
[Q109] How do you choose chunk size?	Empirically: structure-aware chunks with headings, two or three sizes compared on retrieval recall and answer quality.	bank level::intermediate ch::31
[Q110] What is HyDE?	Embedding an LLM-written hypothetical answer to search with, because it resembles documents more than a short question does.	bank level::intermediate ch::31
[Q111] What is contextual retrieval?	Prepending a short LLM-written description of each chunk's place in its document before embedding and BM25 indexing, so chunks are self-explanatory.	bank level::intermediate ch::31
[Q112] RAG answers are wrong 20% of the time. How do you find the cause?	For a sample of failures, check whether the right passage was retrieved, whether it reached the final context, and whether the answer used it; fix the dominant stage.	bank level::senior ch::31
[Q113] When would you use GraphRAG or agentic RAG?	GraphRAG for global or multi-hop questions over a stable entity-rich corpus; agentic RAG for multi-part questions mixing documents and live data. Both cost more; start with a fixed pipeline.	bank level::senior ch::31
[Q114] Workflow or agent?	Workflow when steps are known; agent when the path depends on findings. Prefer the simplest that works.	bank level::basic ch::33
[Q115] What makes a good tool for an agent?	Clear description with units and limits, strict schema, concise output, actionable errors, idempotency, and policy checks inside the tool.	bank level::intermediate ch::33
[Q116] Why do multi-step agents fail more often?	Errors compound: per-step success p gives roughly pⁿ over n steps.	bank level::intermediate ch::33
[Q117] What is MCP?	An open protocol exposing tools, resources and prompts from servers to AI applications through a standard interface.	bank level::intermediate ch::33
[Q118] How do you take an agent to production safely?	Sandbox evaluation on real cases over repeated runs, guardrails and budgets, audit logs, shadow mode against humans, gradual automation by risk tier, monitoring of success, cost and overrides.	bank level::senior ch::33
[Q119] Why are public benchmarks insufficient?	They do not reflect your inputs, criteria or failure costs, and can be contaminated.	bank level::basic ch::24_25
[Q120] Where do you start evaluating a new LLM feature?	Error analysis: read 50–100 traces, write notes, group into failure modes, build an evaluation per frequent mode.	bank level::intermediate ch::24_25
[Q121] How do you validate an LLM judge?	Against expert labels: Cohen's kappa, true positive and true negative rates, compared with human-human agreement, on a held-out set.	bank level::intermediate ch::24_25
[Q122] Name three LLM-judge biases.	Position, verbosity, self-preference.	bank level::intermediate ch::24_25
[Q123] Faithfulness vs correctness?	Faithfulness: claims supported by the retrieved context. Correctness: claims true against a reference. A faithful answer from a wrong document is still wrong.	bank level::intermediate ch::24_25
[Q124] How many examples do you need to compare two prompts?	Depends on the difference: use paired comparisons on the same cases (McNemar); 100 cases give about ±7 points on a single rate near 85% (±10 near 50%), small differences need hundreds.	bank level::senior ch::24_25
[Q125] How do you evaluate an agent?	End-state task success, trajectory checks, efficiency, robustness to tool failures, repeated runs (pass^k), safety tests.	bank level::senior ch::24_25
[Q126] What is hallucination, and how do you reduce it in products?	Confident, unsupported output. Ground on retrieved sources with citations, allow abstention, verify claims, constrain outputs, and hand off when unsure.	bank level::basic ch::28
[Q127] How do you handle PII in an LLM pipeline?	Minimise what is sent, redact or pseudonymise before calls, contractual and regional controls with providers, no PII in logs or training data without consent, output filters.	bank level::intermediate ch::28
[Q128] How would you moderate user reviews with ML?	Policy definitions first; a fast classifier for clear cases, an LLM for nuanced ones, human review for borderline and appeals; precision and recall per policy category, per language; monitoring for new abuse patterns.	bank level::intermediate ch::28
[Q129] What does the EU AI Act mean for a practitioner?	A risk-based framework: prohibited practices, high-risk systems with obligations (risk management, data governance, documentation, human oversight, accuracy and robustness), transparency duties (for example disclosing AI interaction and AI-generated content), and obligations for general-purpose AI model providers. Know which tier your system falls into and document accordingly.	bank level::senior ch::28
[Q130] Why multi-stage recommenders?	Cheap high-recall retrieval over the catalogue, then filtering, then a heavy ranker on hundreds, then page-level re-ranking, to meet latency and cost.	bank level::basic ch::43
[Q131] Implicit vs explicit feedback?	Explicit ratings are rare and biased; implicit behaviour (impressions, clicks, orders) is abundant but has no explicit negatives.	bank level::basic ch::43
[Q132] How does a two-tower model train and serve?	User and item towers trained with in-batch softmax (plus logQ correction); item vectors precomputed into an ANN index; the user vector computed per request.	bank level::intermediate ch::43
[Q133] What is position bias and how do you correct it?	Items shown higher get more clicks regardless of relevance; use position as a training feature removed at serving, or inverse propensity weighting with propensities from randomisation.	bank level::intermediate ch::43
[Q134] How do you handle cold start for a new restaurant?	Content features in the item tower, ID dropout in training, exploration impressions, borrowing from similar restaurants.	bank level::intermediate ch::43
[Q135] Explain Thompson sampling.	Keep a posterior per option (Beta for click rates); sample once from each; pick the highest; update with the outcome.	bank level::intermediate ch::43
[Q136] Offline NDCG up 3%, A/B flat. Why?	Candidate set mismatch, label vs goal mismatch, leakage, position bias in test labels, re-ranking rules overriding, low power, or segment effects.	bank level::senior ch::43
[Q137] Where would you use LLMs in a recommender?	Offline item enrichment and cold-start features, calibrated LLM judges for offline evaluation, conversational surfaces, semantic IDs; not for scoring thousands of candidates per request.	bank level::senior ch::43
[Q138] Pointwise, pairwise and listwise?	Per document, per pair, per list; the last two optimise order directly.	bank level::basic ch::44
[Q139] Explain LambdaMART.	RankNet's pairwise gradients scaled by the |ΔNDCG| of swapping each pair (LambdaRank), fitted by gradient-boosted trees.	bank level::intermediate ch::44
[Q140] Why must ideal DCG use all relevant documents?	Otherwise a system that retrieves few documents is not penalised for what it missed.	bank level::intermediate ch::44
[Q141] How do you handle a navigational query for a closed restaurant?	Show it first, labelled closed with opening time, then similar open alternatives.	bank level::intermediate ch::44
[Q142] What is team-draft interleaving?	Alternately drafting results from two rankers into one list and crediting clicks to the ranker that supplied them; a sensitive comparison.	bank level::intermediate ch::44
[Q143] Search conversion dropped 2%. Debugging plan?	Instrumentation and traffic mix first, then segment by query type, language, city and platform; zero-result and reformulation spikes; supply changes; recent ranking and index changes; compare with browse conversion.	bank level::senior ch::44
[Q144] Why is seasonal naive a strong forecasting baseline?	Delivery demand has strong weekly patterns; the same hour last week captures them for free.	bank level::basic ch::45

[Ch 3] Explain Adam.	Momentum (running mean of gradients) plus per-parameter adaptive step sizes (running mean of squared gradients), with bias correction for the early steps. AdamW applies weight decay separately from the gradient update.	chapter ch::3
[Ch 3] When would you use MAE, MSE or a quantile loss?	MSE when large errors are disproportionately costly (predicts the mean); MAE when robust to outliers (predicts the median); quantile when you need a buffer, such as an ETA you will meet 90% of the time.	chapter ch::3
[Ch 3] How do you validate a model that predicts next week's demand?	Walk-forward validation with expanding or rolling windows, a gap equal to the forecast horizon, features computed point-in-time, metrics per segment, and comparison with seasonal-naive baselines.	chapter ch::3
[Ch 3] How does target encoding work and what can go wrong?	Replace a category with the (smoothed) mean target of that category. Computed naively, each row's own label leaks into its feature; use out-of-fold encoding with smoothing, and past-only data for time series.	chapter ch::3
[Ch 3] Your model has 0.99 AUC offline. What do you do?	Suspect leakage: check feature importances for a dominant feature, review feature timestamps against the prediction moment, check splits by time and entity, look for duplicates, and compare with simple baselines.	chapter ch::3
[Ch 3] What does early stopping do and why does it regularise?	Stops training when validation loss stops improving; it limits how far parameters move from their initial values, restricting effective model capacity.	chapter ch::3
[Ch 3] How do you frame "reduce late deliveries" as an ML problem?	Clarify the decision (promise times, dispatching, restaurant prep alerts), then choose a framing whose output that decision uses: quantile ETA regression for promises, a late-risk classifier for proactive alerts. Define the label (minutes late vs promise), the prediction moment, features available then, error costs, and the baseline.	chapter ch::3
[Ch 3] Your validation metric improves but production does not. What could be wrong with your training setup?	Leakage, splits that do not mirror production (random vs time, entity overlap), a training distribution different from serving (selection bias in logged data), train-serve skew in features, a proxy label misaligned with the business metric.	chapter ch::3
[Ch 3] When should you not use ML?	When rules achieve the goal, when there is no reliable label or feedback loop, when errors are unacceptable and unexplainable, when data is too small, or when nobody will act on the prediction.	chapter ch::3
[Ch 4] Define precision and recall, and give a case where each matters more.	Precision: of the items flagged, the share that are truly positive. Recall: of the true positives, the share flagged. Precision matters more when false alarms are costly (blocking good customers' orders); recall matters more when misses are costly (food-safety complaints, account takeovers).	chapter ch::4
[Ch 4] Why is accuracy a bad metric for fraud detection?	With 1% fraud, predicting "not fraud" for everything is 99% accurate. Accuracy is dominated by the majority class; precision, recall and PR-AUC focus on the rare class you care about.	chapter ch::4
[Ch 4] What does ROC-AUC measure?	The probability that a randomly chosen positive is scored above a randomly chosen negative. It measures ranking quality across all thresholds and ignores calibration.	chapter ch::4
[Ch 4] MAE or RMSE?	MAE is in the target's units, robust to outliers, and is minimised by the median. RMSE punishes large errors more and is minimised by the mean. Choose by the cost of errors: if one 30-minute ETA miss is much worse than three 10-minute misses, RMSE (or a quantile loss) fits better.	chapter ch::4
[Ch 4] What is F1 and when would you use Fβ instead?	The harmonic mean of precision and recall, high only when both are high. Fβ with β > 1 when recall matters more, β < 1 when precision matters more. Better still: choose the threshold with explicit costs.	chapter ch::4
[Ch 4] ROC-AUC or PR-AUC for a problem with 0.5% positives?	PR-AUC, or precision and recall at the operating point. Under heavy imbalance, the false positive rate stays tiny even with many false positives, so ROC-AUC looks great while precision is poor. The PR-AUC baseline is the positive rate (0.005), which makes progress visible.	chapter ch::4
[Ch 4] What is calibration and when does it matter?	Predicted probabilities match observed frequencies. It matters whenever the probability is used as a number: multiplying p(order) by basket value, comparing p(click) × bid across ads, setting expected-cost thresholds, or combining heads of a multi-task model. Measure with a reliability diagram and ECE; fix with Platt, isotonic or temperature scaling on held-out data.	chapter ch::4
[Ch 4] You trained on 10% of negatives. What happens to the predictions and how do you fix them?	They are inflated, because the model saw positives ten times more often than reality. Correct with q = p / (p + (1 − p) / w) with w = 0.1. Ranking (AUC) is unaffected, because the correction is monotone.	chapter ch::4
[Ch 4] Micro, macro or weighted F1 for a 300-class dish taxonomy?	Macro (and per-class) if the long tail matters, because micro is dominated by the frequent classes. Report micro too as an overall volume view, plus a confusion analysis of the most confused class pairs.	chapter ch::4
[Ch 4] How do you choose a classification threshold?	From the business: minimise expected cost with explicit FP and FN costs; or maximise recall subject to a precision floor; or respect a capacity limit (top N per day for manual review). Tune on validation data, and revisit when base rates change.	chapter ch::4
[Ch 4] Why is MAPE dangerous for demand forecasting?	It divides by the actual value, so small actuals (night hours, small zones) explode the error, and it is undefined at zero. WAPE (total absolute error over total actual) is stable and weights errors by volume.	chapter ch::4
[Ch 4] Model B beats model A by 0.003 AUC. Do you ship it?	Not on that alone. Get a confidence interval on the difference with a paired bootstrap over users. Check slices, calibration and latency cost. Then ask whether offline AUC gains have historically translated into online gains for this system; if yes and the difference is significant, run an A/B test.	chapter ch::4
[Ch 4] Your offline metric improved but the A/B test shows no change. What are possible reasons?	Proxy mismatch (clicks vs orders), offline candidate set different from production, leakage or train-serve skew, feedback-loop bias in offline labels, an effect too small for the test's power, or segments moving in opposite directions. Investigate each with data, not opinions.	chapter ch::4
[Ch 4] How do you build the metric set for a new ML feature?	Start from the business goal (north star), choose an online primary metric that reflects it and is sensitive enough to move in a test, add guardrails and counter-metrics, then pick an offline metric and validate that it predicts the online one. Write them down before building the model.	chapter ch::4
[Ch 4] How would you compute a confidence interval for NDCG@10 on a search test set?	NDCG is computed per query and averaged, so bootstrap over queries (or over users, if one user issues many queries): resample them with replacement, recompute the mean NDCG each time, take percentiles. For comparing two rankers, bootstrap the per-query difference.	chapter ch::4
[Ch 5] [B] Why do we need non-linear activations?	Without them, any stack of layers is a single linear function.	chapter ch::5
[Ch 5] [B] Why did ReLU replace sigmoid in hidden layers?	Sigmoid saturates and its gradient is at most 0.25, so gradients vanish through many layers; ReLU has gradient 1 for positive inputs and is cheap.	chapter ch::5
[Ch 5] [I] Derive the gradient of softmax with cross-entropy with respect to the logits.	p − y (divided by n for a batch mean): the softmax Jacobian and the log combine into this simple form.	chapter ch::5
[Ch 5] [I] What problem do Xavier and He initialisation solve?	Keeping activation and gradient variance roughly constant across layers so signals neither vanish nor explode; He accounts for ReLU zeroing half the inputs.	chapter ch::5
[Ch 5] [I] Batch norm vs layer norm, and why transformers use layer norm.	Batch norm normalises across the batch per feature; layer norm across features per example. Layer norm does not depend on batch size or sequence length and behaves the same in training and inference.	chapter ch::5
[Ch 5] [I] How does dropout work at train and test time?	Train: zero each unit with probability p and scale the rest by 1/(1 − p). Test: use all units unchanged.	chapter ch::5
[Ch 5] [I] Why use learning-rate warm-up?	Early gradients and Adam's moment estimates are unreliable; large early steps can push training into bad regions. Warm-up ramps the rate up gently.	chapter ch::5
[Ch 5] [S] Your network's training loss does not decrease. Walk through debugging.	Check data and labels; initial loss vs ln(k); overfit one batch; gradient flow; learning-rate range test; train/eval modes; then capacity and regularisation.	chapter ch::5
[Ch 5] [S] Why is bf16 preferred over fp16 for training large models?	Same exponent range as fp32, so it avoids overflow and underflow without loss scaling, at slightly lower mantissa precision that training tolerates.	chapter ch::5
[Ch 7] Write the AdamW update. Why is weight decay "decoupled"?	See 7.1. With Adam, an L2 term in the gradient is divided by √v, so parameters with large gradients get almost no regularisation; AdamW subtracts lr·wd·p directly, independent of the gradient statistics.	chapter ch::7
[Ch 7] Why do transformers need warm-up?	Adam's first steps have a poorly estimated second moment, so every coordinate moves by about the full learning rate in a random-looking direction; with large rates this throws the model into a bad region or blows up attention logits. Warm-up keeps the early steps small while the statistics settle.	chapter ch::7
[Ch 7] What is the WSD schedule and why do teams like it?	Warm-up, a long constant phase, then a short decay. Any checkpoint in the constant phase can be decayed into a good model, so you can extend runs, run cheap scaling-law and data experiments from branches, and avoid fixing the total length in advance.	chapter ch::7
[Ch 7] What is the critical batch size?	The batch size beyond which larger batches give diminishing reductions in the number of steps. It is roughly the gradient noise scale tr(Σ)/|G|², grows as the loss falls, and is larger for bigger, better-trained models.	chapter ch::7
[Ch 7] How does Muon differ from Adam?	Adam normalises each coordinate separately. Muon orthogonalises the whole momentum matrix of a hidden layer (via Newton–Schulz), equalising the size of the update in all directions. It applies to 2-D hidden matrices only; embeddings and heads stay on AdamW.	chapter ch::7
[Ch 7] What problem does µP solve? State its main rules for Adam.	Hyperparameters, especially the learning rate, stop transferring across widths under the standard parametrisation. µP scales hidden learning rates as 1/width, initialises the output layer with variance 1/width² and scales attention by 1/d, so the per-step change in each layer's output is width-independent and tuned settings transfer from small proxies.	chapter ch::7
[Ch 7] Why bf16 rather than fp16 for training? Why keep fp32 master weights?	bf16 has fp32's exponent range, so gradients do not underflow and no loss scaling is needed. Its 8-bit precision cannot represent small updates to weights of order 1 (1 + 1e-3 rounds to 1), so updates are accumulated in an fp32 copy.	chapter ch::7
[Ch 7] What makes fp8 training hard?	Tiny dynamic range (e4m3 tops out at 448 and flushes values below about 0.002), so tensors need fine-grained scaling factors; some operations and states must stay in higher precision; errors can accumulate silently. Needs a careful recipe and a bf16 baseline.	chapter ch::7
[Ch 7] Your loss spiked at step 60k and did not recover. Walk through what you do.	Restart from a checkpoint before the spike; inspect the batches at the spike; check logged gradient norms, attention max logits and output log Z for the steps before; skip suspicious data and lower the rate; if logits were growing, add QK-norm or z-loss in future runs. Record it, because spikes recur.	chapter ch::7
[Ch 7] What are z-loss and QK-norm for?	z-loss penalises log² of the softmax normaliser to stop output logits drifting; QK-norm normalises queries and keys so attention logits stay bounded. Both prevent the logit growth that causes late-training instability.	chapter ch::7
[Ch 7] What is the edge of stability?	Full-batch GD increases sharpness until it hovers at 2/η, where classical analysis predicts divergence, while the loss still falls non-monotonically. It shows that the learning rate shapes which minima are reachable.	chapter ch::7
[Ch 7] A paper claims a new optimiser trains 2× faster. What do you check?	That the Adam baseline was tuned as hard (learning rate, β₂, warm-up, weight decay) at the same batch size and schedule; that the comparison is at matched tokens and final decayed loss, across several scales and seeds; and that the gain holds on downstream evaluation, not only on loss.	chapter ch::7
[Ch 10] Explain self-attention.	Each position computes a query, key and value by linear projections; attention weights are the softmax of scaled query-key dot products; each output is the weighted sum of values. It lets every token gather information from relevant tokens anywhere in the (allowed) context.	chapter ch::10
[Ch 10] Why divide by √dₖ?	Dot products of dₖ-dimensional vectors have variance growing with dₖ; large scores saturate softmax and shrink gradients. Scaling keeps them in a trainable range.	chapter ch::10
[Ch 10] Why multiple heads?	Each head can attend to different relations (syntax, coreference, position) in its own subspace; concatenating them is more expressive than one head of the same total size, at the same cost.	chapter ch::10
[Ch 10] What is the causal mask?	A mask setting scores for future positions to −∞, so each token only attends to itself and earlier tokens; required for next-token training and generation.	chapter ch::10
[Ch 10] BERT vs GPT?	BERT: encoder, bidirectional attention, masked-token objective; best for understanding tasks and embeddings. GPT: decoder, causal attention, next-token objective; best for generation.	chapter ch::10
[Ch 10] Why do transformers need positional encodings, and how does RoPE work?	Attention ignores order. RoPE rotates queries and keys by position-dependent angles so their dot product depends on relative distance, which works naturally with caching; running beyond the trained length still needs scaling tricks (position interpolation, NTK, YaRN) and some long-context fine-tuning.	chapter ch::10
[Ch 10] Pre-norm vs post-norm?	Pre-norm normalises inside the residual branch, keeping the residual path an identity: much more stable training for deep models; post-norm needs careful warm-up.	chapter ch::10
[Ch 10] What is the KV cache and how big is it?	Stored keys and values of past tokens per layer, so decoding computes only the new token. Size: 2 × layers × KV heads × head dim × tokens × bytes. For a 32-layer model with 32 KV heads of 128 at 8k tokens in fp16, about 4.3 GB per sequence.	chapter ch::10
[Ch 10] What do MQA and GQA change?	They share key/value heads across query heads, shrinking the KV cache (and memory bandwidth per decoding step) by the sharing factor, allowing larger batches and longer contexts; GQA keeps quality close to full multi-head attention.	chapter ch::10
[Ch 10] What does FlashAttention do?	Computes exact attention in tiles that fit in on-chip memory, with an online softmax, never materialising the T×T matrix; reduces memory traffic, so it is faster and memory-linear in T.	chapter ch::10
[Ch 10] What is the computational complexity of a transformer layer?	Attention O(T²d), FFN O(Td²); attention dominates for long sequences, the FFN for short ones.	chapter ch::10
[Ch 10] Why did decoder-only models win for general-purpose LLMs?	One self-supervised objective on any text, smooth scaling with data and compute, every task expressible as continuation, simple efficient inference with KV caching, and instruction tuning on top.	chapter ch::10
[Ch 10] How do you extend a model's context length?	RoPE scaling (position interpolation, NTK-aware, YaRN) plus fine-tuning on long sequences; efficient attention kernels; GQA and cache quantisation for memory; then evaluate on realistic long-context tasks, not only needle tests.	chapter ch::10
[Ch 10] Why is decoding memory-bound while prefill is compute-bound?	Prefill processes many tokens per weight load (large matrix multiplications); decode processes one token per sequence per step, so each step reads all weights and the cache for little compute. Batching more sequences raises arithmetic intensity, which is why continuous batching matters.	chapter ch::10
[Ch 10] Walk me through what happens when an LLM generates one token.	The new token is embedded; in each layer, it is normalised, projected to Q, K, V (RoPE on Q, K), K and V appended to the cache, attention computed over the cache, added to the residual, then the FFN; after the last layer, normalisation and the output projection give logits; sampling (temperature, top-p) picks the token.	chapter ch::10
[Ch 10] How many parameters does a decoder have, roughly, and where are they?	Per layer about 4d² for attention (less with GQA) and 8d² for a SwiGLU FFN of matched size, so about 12d² per layer, plus 2 × vocabulary × d for untied embeddings. For d = 4,096, 32 layers and a 128k vocabulary: about 8B, 70% in the FFNs.	chapter ch::10
[Ch 10] Explain the online softmax behind FlashAttention.	Keep a running maximum, normaliser and weighted value sum per query; when a tile raises the maximum, rescale the accumulated sums by exp(m_old − m_new); divide at the end. The result is exact, and only one tile of scores exists at a time.	chapter ch::10
[Ch 11] How does a top-k MoE layer work, and what does it change in compute and memory?	A router scores experts per token; the token goes through the top k experts and their outputs are mixed. Compute per token scales with k, memory with the total number of experts; training and serving need expert parallelism and all-to-all communication.	chapter ch::11
[Ch 11] Why do MoE models need load balancing? Give two approaches.	Routers collapse onto a few experts. The Switch/GShard auxiliary loss E·Σ f_e P_e pushes towards uniform load; auxiliary-loss-free balancing adjusts a per-expert selection bias up or down by load, avoiding gradient interference.	chapter ch::11
[Ch 11] What are fine-grained and shared experts?	Many small experts with more of them active, giving more combinations; and one or more always-on experts holding common knowledge so routed ones specialise. Introduced in DeepSeekMoE and now standard.	chapter ch::11
[Ch 11] Explain MLA. Why does it need decoupled RoPE?	Keys and values are reconstructed from a cached low-rank latent per token, cutting the cache by tens of times. The up-projections are absorbed into the query and output matrices at inference, which only works if no position-dependent rotation sits between them, so position goes through a small extra set of rotary dimensions.	chapter ch::11
[Ch 11] Show that causal linear attention is an RNN.	With φ(q)·φ(k) instead of exp(q·k), the sums Σ φ(k_s) v_sᵀ and Σ φ(k_s) can be accumulated step by step; each output needs only the current state.	chapter ch::11
[Ch 11] What is the main weakness of SSMs and linear attention? How do hybrids address it?	A fixed-size state cannot store everything, so precise recall from long context degrades. Hybrids keep a few full-attention layers for retrieval and make the rest linear-time.	chapter ch::11
[Ch 11] What does the delta rule add to linear attention?	It subtracts the state's current prediction for a key before writing the new value, so it overwrites associations instead of summing them; combined with a gate it can forget.	chapter ch::11
[Ch 11] What is multi-token prediction good for?	Extra training signal from predicting several tokens ahead, and built-in draft heads for speculative decoding.	chapter ch::11
[Ch 11] A paper says its linear-time model beats a transformer at 350M parameters. What do you ask?	Same data and tokens? Modern, tuned baseline? Does it hold at larger scale? Recall and long-context evaluations? Real throughput with kernels? Seeds?	chapter ch::11
[Ch 11] When would you choose a dense model over an MoE?	Small deployments where memory, not compute, is the constraint; on-device; heavy fine-tuning by teams without MoE expertise; very low batch sizes where experts are under-used.	chapter ch::11
[Ch 13] Derive the compute-optimal model size from L = E + A/N^α + B/D^β and C = 6ND.	Substitute D = C/6N, differentiate, set to zero: N_opt ∝ C^(β/(α+β)), D_opt ∝ C^(α/(α+β)). Equal exponents mean equal growth.	chapter ch::13
[Ch 13] Why did Kaplan and Chinchilla disagree?	Kaplan read intermediate checkpoints of runs with a fixed long schedule, which under-estimates short, fully-decayed runs and biases towards bigger models; they also excluded embedding parameters and used smaller models. Chinchilla matched schedules to run length.	chapter ch::13
[Ch 13] Why do labs train 8B models on 15T tokens?	Inference cost. Minimising training plus lifetime serving cost favours smaller models trained much longer than Chinchilla-optimal.	chapter ch::13
[Ch 13] How would you fit a scaling law for a new architecture?	IsoFLOP profiles over 5+ budgets spanning two orders of magnitude, fully decayed runs, tuned or µP-transferred learning rates, several seeds at small scale, fit the optimum's power law, validate on held-out larger runs, compare slopes and offsets with a tuned transformer baseline.	chapter ch::13
[Ch 13] What is the value of repeating data?	Roughly as good as new data up to about 4 epochs, rapidly diminishing beyond about 16 (Muennighoff et al.); repeat high-quality sources preferentially.	chapter ch::13
[Ch 13] Are emergent abilities real?	Many apparent jumps come from discontinuous metrics like exact match; with continuous metrics the same tasks improve smoothly. Some multi-step abilities still appear abruptly, so treat forecasts with wide intervals.	chapter ch::13
[Ch 13] How can you predict a benchmark score before a big run?	Train a ladder of small models, fit accuracy as a function of loss (sigmoid), fit loss as a function of compute, chain the two.	chapter ch::13
[Ch 13] Explain the unbiased pass@k estimator and why you need it.	With n samples and c correct, pass@k = 1 − C(n−c,k)/C(n,k). Plugging the sample rate into 1 − (1−p)^k is biased for small n.	chapter ch::13
[Ch 13] What is test-time compute scaling?	Accuracy improving with inference compute (more samples, search, longer reasoning). For hard problems it can substitute for model size, if there is a good way to verify or select answers.	chapter ch::13
[Ch 13] A colleague's scaling curve bends at the largest size. Possible causes?	The learning rate or batch size not re-tuned for that size, a schedule mismatch, data running out (repetition), numerical instability, approaching the irreducible loss, or a bug in that run. Check hyperparameters first.	chapter ch::13
[Ch 14] How does an image become tokens in a ViT?	Cut it into p × p patches, flatten and linearly project each, add positions. 224² with 16-pixel patches gives 196 tokens.	chapter ch::14
[Ch 14] Compare CLIP and SigLIP losses.	CLIP: softmax cross-entropy over the batch in both directions. SigLIP: an independent sigmoid per image–text pair with a learned bias. SigLIP needs no batch-wide normalisation, so it scales to large batches cheaply and works better at small ones.	chapter ch::14
[Ch 14] Describe the LLaVA architecture and training.	Frozen vision encoder, MLP projector into the LLM's embedding space, image tokens spliced into the sequence. Stage 1 trains the projector on captions; stage 2 instruction-tunes with the LLM unfrozen.	chapter ch::14
[Ch 14] How do VLMs handle high-resolution documents?	Tiling with an overview image, or native-resolution encoding with 2-D RoPE; then token merging (pixel shuffle) or a resampler to fit the token budget.	chapter ch::14
[Ch 14] Cross-attention versus projector-in-sequence designs?	Cross-attention leaves the LLM's text path untouched and keeps image features out of the context; projector designs are simpler, reuse the LLM fully and handle interleaving naturally, at the cost of context length.	chapter ch::14
[Ch 14] What is early fusion, and why is it attractive?	Images become discrete tokens in the same vocabulary, so one model reads and generates both. Costs: the tokenizer loses detail and training is harder to stabilise.	chapter ch::14
[Ch 14] How would you detect that a VLM relies on language priors?	Blind baselines (image removed), counterfactual images that contradict the prior, hallucination probes for absent objects.	chapter ch::14
[Ch 14] Explain classifier-free guidance.	Train with the condition randomly dropped; at sampling, push the prediction away from the unconditional one: ε_u + w(ε_c − ε_u).	chapter ch::14
[Ch 14] How does Whisper process audio?	Log-mel spectrogram (10 ms hop), conv downsampling, transformer encoder; a decoder generates text with special tokens for language, task and timestamps.	chapter ch::14
[Ch 14] What limits video understanding?	Token count. Sparse and adaptive frame sampling, spatio-temporal token merging, 3-D positions, and memory or retrieval for long videos.	chapter ch::14
[Ch 16] Walk through a web-data pipeline.	URL filtering, HTML extraction, language ID, heuristic filters, deduplication, quality classifier, PII and toxicity filtering, decontamination, tokenisation and sharding with metadata. Cheap steps first.	chapter ch::16
[Ch 16] How does MinHash LSH find near-duplicates?	Shingle documents; for each of n hash functions keep the minimum hash; agreement rate estimates Jaccard similarity. Split signatures into b bands of r rows; pairs matching on any band are candidates with probability 1 − (1 − sʳ)ᵇ, which sets the threshold.	chapter ch::16
[Ch 16] How would you build a quality classifier, and what can go wrong?	Positive examples from a trusted reference (or LLM ratings of samples), negatives from random web; a cheap model (fastText) scores everything; keep top buckets. Risks: narrowing style and topics, bias against dialects or regions, over-fitting to benchmark-like text.	chapter ch::16
[Ch 16] Why deduplicate, and can you overdo it?	Saves compute, reduces memorisation and contamination. Overdoing it (for example global deduplication across many snapshots) can remove good recurring documents and leave low-quality unique ones.	chapter ch::16
[Ch 16] How do you choose a data mixture?	Small-scale ablations on proxy models, learned weighting (DoReMi), or fitted mixing laws, constrained by how many epochs each source can afford; validate per domain.	chapter ch::16
[Ch 16] What is annealing on high-quality data, and how can you use it as a measurement tool?	Switching to curated data during the final learning-rate decay. Annealing a checkpoint with and without a candidate source is a cheap test of its value.	chapter ch::16
[Ch 16] How do you decontaminate, and what are the limits?	N-gram overlap (around 13-grams) with test sets, plus embedding or LLM checks for paraphrases. Misses translations, paraphrases and contamination via synthetic data.	chapter ch::16
[Ch 16] What are the risks of synthetic pretraining data?	Narrowing, inherited errors, contamination, and collapse if models train on their own outputs repeatedly without fresh data.	chapter ch::16
[Ch 16] How do you make data ablations trustworthy?	Proxy models large and long enough, evaluations with continuous signals, seeds for a noise floor, one change at a time, check that rankings agree across two proxy scales.	chapter ch::16
[Ch 16] How would you improve a model's performance in a low-resource language?	Better language ID and extraction for that language, language-specific quality filters, upsampling within epoch limits, translated and synthetic data with verification, tokenizer coverage (Chapter 9), and evaluation by native speakers.	chapter ch::16
[Ch 17] Estimate the time to train a 70B model on 15T tokens on 4,096 GPUs.	6 × 70e9 × 15e12 = 6.3e24 FLOPs; 4,096 × ~1e15 FLOP/s × 0.4 MFU ≈ 1.6e18 FLOP/s; about 45 days.	chapter ch::17
[Ch 17] How much memory does training a 7B model with Adam need?	16 bytes per parameter = 112 GB, plus activations: more than one 80 GB GPU, hence ZeRO/FSDP or more GPUs.	chapter ch::17
[Ch 17] Explain ZeRO stages 1–3.	Shard optimiser states (1), plus gradients (2), plus weights (3). Stages 1 and 2 add no communication; stage 3 gathers weights per layer, about 1.5× DDP's traffic, hidden by prefetching.	chapter ch::17
[Ch 17] How does ring all-reduce work, and what does it cost?	Reduce-scatter then all-gather around a ring; each GPU sends about twice the data size, independent of the number of GPUs.	chapter ch::17
[Ch 17] How does Megatron split an MLP across GPUs?	First matrix by columns, second by rows; each GPU computes its hidden slice independently; one all-reduce sums the outputs. Attention splits by heads.	chapter ch::17
[Ch 17] Why keep tensor parallelism inside a node?	It needs an all-reduce of activations in every layer, forward and backward, so it needs NVLink-class bandwidth.	chapter ch::17
[Ch 17] What is the pipeline bubble, and how do you reduce it?	Idle time at the start and end of each batch, (p−1)/(m+p−1). More micro-batches, interleaved schedules, zero-bubble schedules.	chapter ch::17
[Ch 17] How does context parallelism handle attention?	Each GPU holds a block of the sequence; key–value blocks pass round a ring while each GPU computes attention for its queries, with load balancing for the causal mask.	chapter ch::17
[Ch 17] What is MFU, and what is a good value?	Model FLOPs achieved per second over peak; 35–50% for well-tuned dense training, lower for MoE.	chapter ch::17
[Ch 17] Your run's throughput dropped 20% overnight. What do you check?	Per-rank step times for stragglers, node health and network errors, data-loader stalls (I/O), checkpoint timing, recent configuration or data changes, thermal throttling.	chapter ch::17
[Ch 17] How often should you checkpoint?	Balance checkpoint cost against expected lost work: √(2·C·M); make checkpoints sharded and asynchronous to make C small.	chapter ch::17
[Ch 18] What makes a good SFT dataset?	Diverse prompts covering target skills and formats, high-quality verified responses, both refusals and non-refusals done right, multi-turn data, deduplicated and decontaminated. Quality beats volume for format; skills need volume.	chapter ch::18
[Ch 18] Write the Bradley–Terry reward-model loss and explain its assumptions.	−log σ(r_c − r_r). Assumes preferences come from a single scalar reward per response plus logistic noise; ignores ties, intransitive and context-dependent preferences.	chapter ch::18
[Ch 18] Walk through PPO for LLMs.	Sample responses; score them with the reward model; add a per-token KL penalty to the reference; compute advantages with a value model and GAE; update with the clipped ratio objective for a few epochs; repeat.	chapter ch::18
[Ch 18] Why clip the probability ratio?	The data was sampled by the old policy; large policy changes make the advantage estimates invalid. Clipping removes the incentive to move the ratio beyond 1 ± ε.	chapter ch::18
[Ch 18] Derive DPO in a few lines.	The KL-regularised optimum is π_ref·exp(r/β)/Z. Solve for r, substitute into Bradley–Terry; Z cancels; the loss depends only on log-ratios of policy to reference.	chapter ch::18
[Ch 18] If DPO and RLHF share an optimum, why do they behave differently?	DPO fits a fixed offline dataset; PPO samples on-policy and can explore. Distribution shift, finite data and likelihood displacement make offline DPO weaker; iterative on-policy DPO narrows the gap.	chapter ch::18
[Ch 18] What is reward over-optimisation and how do you detect and limit it?	Proxy reward keeps rising while true quality falls. Detect with a gold judge or humans on checkpoints at increasing KL; limit with KL penalties, early stopping, better or ensembled reward models, length control.	chapter ch::18
[Ch 18] Your DPO run increases the margin but the chosen log-probability falls. Is that a problem?	It is likelihood displacement: mass moves to other responses. Check generation quality; add an SFT term on chosen responses, lower the learning rate, or use on-policy pairs.	chapter ch::18
[Ch 18] When would you choose KTO over DPO?	When feedback is binary per response (thumbs up/down, accept/reject) and pairs are not available.	chapter ch::18
[Ch 18] What are the risks of AI feedback?	Judge biases (self-preference, length, position), shared blind spots, and reward hacking against the judge. Audit against human labels, rotate judges, control for length.	chapter ch::18
[Ch 18] How would you evaluate a new post-trained checkpoint before release?	Instruction-following, length-controlled chat quality, capability regression suite, safety and over-refusal, calibration, plus a human evaluation sample; compare with the previous checkpoint with confidence intervals.	chapter ch::18
[Ch 18] Derive the policy gradient.	J = E_(y∼π)[R(y)]; ∇J = Σ_y ∇π(y) R(y) = Σ_y π(y) ∇log π(y) R(y) = E[R(y) ∇log π(y)]. For a sequence, ∇log π(y) is the sum of per-token log-probability gradients. No gradient of the reward is needed.	chapter ch::18
[Ch 18] Why does a baseline not bias the gradient, and which baseline is best?	E[b ∇log π(y)] = b ∇Σ_y π(y) = 0 for any b that does not depend on the sampled y. Variance is lowest near the expected reward: a learned value (PPO), a group mean (GRPO) or a leave-one-out mean (RLOO, exactly unbiased; the plain group mean shrinks the gradient by (N − 1)/N).	chapter ch::18
[Ch 18] On-policy versus off-policy: why can't PPO reuse old rollouts indefinitely?	Off-policy reuse needs importance weights; for a sequence they are products of per-token ratios whose variance grows exponentially with length. PPO uses per-token ratios, clips them, and runs only a few epochs per batch so the data stays nearly on-policy.	chapter ch::18
[Ch 18] Why do LLMs use policy-gradient methods rather than Q-learning?	Huge action space (the vocabulary) at every step, a strong pretrained policy to start from, and a sparse terminal reward. Policy gradients improve the existing policy directly; Q-learning would need to learn values for 100k+ actions per state from scratch.	chapter ch::18
[Ch 19] How was DeepSeek-R1 trained?	Strong base model; R1-Zero: RL with GRPO and rule-based correctness and format rewards, no SFT; R1: a small cold-start SFT on readable long traces, reasoning RL, then rejection-sampled SFT and a general RL stage; then distillation into small models.	chapter ch::19
[Ch 19] Explain GRPO and why it has no value model.	Sample a group per prompt; the advantage is the reward relative to the group mean (often divided by its std); PPO-style clipped updates. The group mean is the baseline, so no learned critic.	chapter ch::19
[Ch 19] What is the length bias in the original GRPO loss?	Per-response averaging gives each token weight A/|o|, so long wrong answers are penalised less per token, encouraging length growth on failures. Fix: token-level averaging or a constant normaliser.	chapter ch::19
[Ch 19] What does DAPO change?	Clip-higher (asymmetric clipping), dynamic sampling (drop groups with no reward variance), token-level loss, overlong reward shaping, no KL penalty.	chapter ch::19
[Ch 19] Why do groups where all answers are right (or wrong) matter?	Zero advantage, zero gradient. At extreme pass rates most groups give no signal; filter by difficulty or resample.	chapter ch::19
[Ch 19] Does RL teach models new reasoning?	Evidence is mixed: RL raises pass@1 and often lowers pass@k at large k, suggesting sharpening of existing solutions; longer, more exploratory RL may extend the frontier; distillation from stronger models adds capability. Measure pass@k curves.	chapter ch::19
[Ch 19] PRM versus ORM?	ORMs (or verifiers) judge final answers; PRMs judge each step. PRMs give denser signal and better reranking and search but are expensive to label and easy to hack as RL rewards.	chapter ch::19
[Ch 19] When does self-consistency fail?	When a wrong answer is more probable than the right one: voting then amplifies the error. It also needs comparable final answers.	chapter ch::19
[Ch 19] How would you reduce overthinking on easy questions?	Length penalties relative to group or difficulty, adaptive budgets, training on mixed difficulty with rewards for efficiency, routing easy queries to a non-thinking mode.	chapter ch::19
[Ch 19] How do you make a 7B model reason well cheaply?	Distil verified long reasoning traces from a stronger model (SFT), then optionally a short RL stage on verifiable tasks at appropriate difficulty.	chapter ch::19
[Ch 19] Why is RL for reasoning expensive, and how do systems handle it?	Generation of long rollouts dominates; separate inference engines, asynchronous pipelines with bounded staleness, length-aware batching, careful handling of probability mismatches between engine and trainer.	chapter ch::19
[Ch 19] What is the training–inference mismatch in RL, and how do you handle it?	The rollout engine and the trainer give slightly different probabilities for the same tokens, so the data is off-policy. Recompute log-probabilities in the trainer, apply truncated importance weights, keep the output head in fp32, monitor the per-token gap, or use batch-invariant kernels.	chapter ch::19
[Ch 19] GRPO, GSPO and CISPO: what differs?	Same group-relative advantage. GRPO clips per-token ratios; GSPO uses one length-normalised sequence ratio and clips whole responses (more stable for MoE); CISPO clips the importance weight but keeps the gradient of every token, so rare high-ratio tokens still learn.	chapter ch::19
[Ch 19] How would you do RL where answers can't be checked?	Per-prompt rubrics scored by an LLM judge (weighted share of criteria met), generative reward models, or pairwise preferences; audit high-reward samples for judge exploitation and length bias, and validate the judge against expert labels.	chapter ch::19
[Ch 19] What is on-policy distillation and when would you use it?	Sample from the student, score each token by the teacher (reverse KL as a dense reward). Use it to make a small model match a strong teacher cheaply, or to recover skills lost after domain fine-tuning; it needs teacher log-probabilities on a shared vocabulary.	chapter ch::19
[Ch 19] How do you compare two RL recipes fairly?	Same model, prompts, verifier and generation budget; several runs if affordable; fit a saturating curve of reward against compute and compare asymptote and efficiency, not one checkpoint; report pass@k curves and held-out evaluations.	chapter ch::19
[Ch 20] When would you fine-tune instead of prompting or using RAG?	For consistent format or style at scale, narrow high-volume tasks where a small fine-tuned model replaces an expensive one, or domain language the base model handles poorly. Not for adding changing facts (RAG) or when prompting already meets the bar.	chapter ch::20
[Ch 20] Explain LoRA.	Freeze W; learn ΔW = B·A with rank r much smaller than the dimensions; output Wx + (α/r)BAx. B starts at zero so training starts from the base model. Trains well under 1% of parameters; merge afterwards for zero latency cost.	chapter ch::20
[Ch 20] What do rank and alpha do?	Rank sets the capacity of the update; alpha scales it (by α/r), keeping the update's magnitude comparable across ranks so learning rates transfer.	chapter ch::20
[Ch 20] What is QLoRA?	LoRA on a frozen base quantised to 4-bit NF4 with double quantisation and paged optimisers, so large models fine-tune on one GPU with quality close to 16-bit LoRA.	chapter ch::20
[Ch 20] Prompt tuning vs prefix-tuning vs LoRA?	Prompt tuning learns soft input embeddings only; prefix-tuning learns key/value prefixes at every layer; LoRA learns low-rank updates to weights. LoRA is the most robust across model sizes and adds no inference latency once merged; prompt tuning is very parameter-cheap but weaker on small models.	chapter ch::20
[Ch 20] Which modules would you target with LoRA, and why?	All linear projections in attention and MLP blocks usually work best for a given budget; attention-only (q, v) is the original minimal setup. Choose by evaluation.	chapter ch::20
[Ch 20] How much memory does fine-tuning a 7B model need?	Full fine-tuning with Adam in mixed precision: about 16 bytes per parameter, roughly 112 GB plus activations. LoRA on a bf16 base: about 14 GB plus activations. QLoRA: about 3.5 GB for weights plus activations and adapters.	chapter ch::20
[Ch 20] What is catastrophic forgetting and how do you limit it?	Loss of general abilities after narrow fine-tuning. Limit with PEFT, low learning rates, fewer epochs, replay of general data, and a regression suite.	chapter ch::20
[Ch 20] How do you build training data for a fine-tune?	Real inputs where possible, outputs from experts or a strong model, filtered for quality and format, deduplicated, balanced across cases, with a separate human-verified evaluation set.	chapter ch::20
[Ch 20] You need a classifier for 50 million merchant descriptions a month. Plan it.	Label a stratified sample with a strong LLM (batch API), audit a few hundred by hand, fine-tune a small encoder or a small LLM with LoRA on the LLM labels, compare with TF-IDF and prompted baselines, route low-confidence cases to the LLM, monitor drift and re-label periodically.	chapter ch::20
[Ch 20] Your fine-tuned model scores higher on the task but users complain it got worse. What happened?	The evaluation set may not represent real traffic; general abilities or tone regressed; it overfits training phrasing; the chat template or decoding settings differ in production. Compare on production samples and run the regression suite.	chapter ch::20
[Ch 20] How would you serve 20 different LoRA adapters efficiently?	One base model in GPU memory with multi-LoRA serving (adapters loaded per request and batched together), adapters cached by popularity, merged models only for the highest-traffic adapters.	chapter ch::20
[Ch 20] Why might LoRA match full fine-tuning on some tasks and not others?	Narrow tasks need low-rank changes, which LoRA captures; large distribution shifts (new languages, continued pre-training on lots of domain text, complex skills) need higher-rank updates, where full fine-tuning or high-rank LoRA does better.	chapter ch::20
[Ch 22] What makes a good prompt for a classification task?	Precise label definitions with examples, boundary cases, an "other" or "unsure" option, a strict output format (enum in a schema), and context separated clearly from instructions.	chapter ch::22
[Ch 22] Zero-shot vs few-shot: when do you add examples?	When instructions alone leave format or boundary cases ambiguous. Use examples that cover hard cases with balanced labels; consider retrieving similar examples per input.	chapter ch::22
[Ch 22] What is chain-of-thought prompting and when is it worth it?	Asking the model to reason before answering. Worth it for multi-step reasoning tasks; wasteful for simple extraction or classification. Reasoning models do it internally, controlled by an effort or token budget.	chapter ch::22
[Ch 22] How do you get reliable JSON out of an LLM?	Structured-output or tool-calling modes with a JSON schema (constrained decoding), Pydantic validation, one retry with the error message, and a fallback path. Nullable fields where data may be missing.	chapter ch::22
[Ch 22] How do you version and test prompts?	Version template, model and parameters together; keep a labelled evaluation set; run it on every change in CI; accept changes only without regressions on key slices; log versions with outputs.	chapter ch::22
[Ch 22] What is "lost in the middle" and what do you do about it?	Models use information at the start and end of long contexts better than in the middle. Retrieve fewer, better passages; put the most relevant first or last; place the question after the context; test at realistic lengths.	chapter ch::22
[Ch 22] When would you choose long context over RAG?	Small, stable corpus needed as a whole, low query volume. RAG for large or changing corpora, high volume, per-document permissions. Often a combination.	chapter ch::22
[Ch 22] How does prompt caching work and how do you design for it?	The provider reuses computation for an identical prompt prefix and bills it at a discount. Put stable content first, keep it byte-identical, put per-request data last.	chapter ch::22
[Ch 22] What is indirect prompt injection?	Instructions hidden in content the system processes (documents, emails, web pages, tool output) that the model may follow. Dangerous for RAG and agents with tools.	chapter ch::22
[Ch 22] Design defences for an assistant that reads customer emails and can issue refunds.	The model drafts; refunds go through a policy engine with limits in code; refunds above a threshold need human approval; email content is treated as untrusted data (quarantined reader model producing structured fields); tools scoped per customer; injection red-team set in CI; monitoring of refund rates.	chapter ch::22
[Ch 22] Your classification prompt scores 88% on the eval set. How do you improve it systematically?	Error analysis: read and categorise the failures (ambiguous labels, missing context, format errors, genuinely hard cases); fix the largest category first (often definitions or examples); re-run; check slices for regressions. Consider dynamic few-shot, a stronger model only for low-confidence cases, or fine-tuning if the error pattern is consistent.	chapter ch::22
[Ch 22] A new model version is released. How do you decide whether to switch?	Run the full evaluation set with the current prompt and with light prompt adjustments; compare quality per slice, latency and cost; check structured-output reliability and safety tests; shadow-test on production traffic; switch with a flag and monitor.	chapter ch::22
[Ch 22] How would you cut the cost of an LLM feature by 5× without losing quality?	Measure where tokens go; shorten and cache the stable prefix; trim retrieved context; route easy cases to a smaller model (cascade); batch offline work; cache repeated requests; distil high-volume tasks into a small model. Verify each step on the evaluation set.	chapter ch::22
[Ch 23] Model B beats A by 1.5 points on a 1,000-question benchmark. Is B better?	Compute the paired difference and its standard error: with ~70% accuracy and moderate disagreement, the SE of the difference is around 1–1.5 points, so 1.5 points is not convincing. Check clustering, prompt-format and seed variance, and contamination.	chapter ch::23
[Ch 23] Why cluster standard errors, and how?	Questions that share a passage, repository or template are correlated; the naive SE understates uncertainty. Sum residuals within clusters, then compute the variance across clusters.	chapter ch::23
[Ch 23] How many questions do you need to detect a 2-point improvement?	For paired comparisons, about 7.84 × d / 0.02² where d is the disagreement rate: roughly 2,000 questions at d = 10%.	chapter ch::23
[Ch 23] Why use bits per byte?	Loss per token depends on the tokenizer; bits per byte normalises by text length and allows comparisons across tokenizers.	chapter ch::23
[Ch 23] How would you detect contamination in a closed model?	Perturbation gaps with significance tests, guided completion, likelihood-based tests, and time-split evaluations on post-cut-off items.	chapter ch::23
[Ch 23] What is IRT and how is it used in LLM evaluation?	Models item difficulty and discrimination and model ability; used to choose small informative subsets, find mislabelled items and place models on a common scale.	chapter ch::23
[Ch 23] What are the known problems with arena leaderboards?	Style and length biases, selection effects from testing private variants, unequal sampling, prompt distribution mismatch, overlapping intervals.	chapter ch::23
[Ch 23] How do you make an LLM judge trustworthy?	Swap positions and average, control for length, use a different model family or several judges, provide references, validate against human labels per category with intervals.	chapter ch::23
[Ch 23] Why might a benchmark lack construct validity?	Items solvable by shortcuts, answer-format artefacts, label errors, or measuring a narrow skill under a broad name. Read items, test blind or perturbed baselines.	chapter ch::23
[Ch 23] What is METR's time-horizon metric?	The length of tasks (in human expert time) a model completes with 50% success; it tracks agentic capability over time in interpretable units.	chapter ch::23
[Ch 24] Why are public benchmarks not enough to choose a model for your product?	They test generic tasks, may be contaminated, and ignore your inputs, criteria and failure costs. Use them to shortlist, then evaluate candidates on your own set.	chapter ch::24
[Ch 24] How do you start evaluating a new LLM feature?	Error analysis: collect 50–100 realistic traces, read and annotate failures, group them into failure modes, count them, then build code checks or calibrated judges for the most frequent modes.	chapter ch::24
[Ch 24] What is LLM-as-judge, and its main formats?	Using an LLM to grade outputs against criteria: pointwise (one output), pairwise (which of two), reference-guided (against a known answer). Best as binary verdicts per criterion with a critique.	chapter ch::24
[Ch 24] Exact match vs token F1?	Exact match requires the normalised strings to be identical; token F1 gives partial credit for word overlap. Both suit short answers with a reference; neither suits open-ended generation.	chapter ch::24
[Ch 24] What biases do LLM judges have and how do you handle them?	Position, verbosity, self-preference, and slice-dependent leniency. Swap orders in pairwise judging, penalise padding, use a different model family, and calibrate against human labels per slice.	chapter ch::24
[Ch 24] How do you validate an LLM judge?	Expert labels on 100–200 outputs for the same criterion; measure kappa and the judge's TPR and TNR; compare with human-human agreement; iterate on the judge prompt; keep a held-out set; re-check after judge or model changes.	chapter ch::24
[Ch 24] Your judge agrees with humans 92% of the time. Is it good?	Not necessarily: if 90% of outputs pass, a judge that always says pass agrees 90%. Check kappa and especially the TNR: the share of real failures it catches.	chapter ch::24
[Ch 24] How many examples do you need in an evaluation set?	Enough for the precision you need: 100 cases gives roughly ±7 points on a pass rate near 85% (±10 near 50%); detecting small differences between systems needs paired comparisons on the same cases or many hundreds of examples.	chapter ch::24
[Ch 24] How do you compare two prompts fairly?	Same cases, same judge, paired test (McNemar on per-case pass/fail), intervals, per-slice results, plus cost and latency.	chapter ch::24
[Ch 24] Design the evaluation for a customer-support RAG assistant.	Error analysis on real tickets; code checks (format, citations exist, language, no PII, policy caps); calibrated judges for faithfulness, answer correctness against references for common questions, and correct abstention; retrieval metrics separately (Chapter 25); per-country and per-language slices; production sampling with daily judging; online metrics (resolution without escalation, re-contact rate, CSAT) with cost and latency guardrails.	chapter ch::24
[Ch 24] Judge scores went down 5 points after a model upgrade of the judge itself. What happened?	The measuring instrument changed: the new judge may be stricter or differently biased. Re-calibrate against the human labels before interpreting any trend; pin judge versions and change them deliberately.	chapter ch::24
[Ch 24] How do you evaluate when there is no single right answer (for example writing restaurant descriptions)?	Define concrete criteria from error analysis (factual consistency with menu data, no invented claims, length, tone, banned phrases), code checks where possible, calibrated binary judges for the rest, pairwise preference tests between versions, and human review samples.	chapter ch::24
[Ch 24] How would you convince a product manager that an LLM feature is ready to launch?	Show per-failure-mode pass rates with intervals on a representative set, critical slices, judge calibration evidence, cost and latency, a comparison with the current process, the monitoring plan, and the rollout plan with guardrails and rollback criteria.	chapter ch::24
[Ch 25] How do you evaluate a RAG system?	By component and end to end: retrieval (context recall and precision at the final context), generation (faithfulness, answer relevance), correctness against references, abstention on out-of-scope questions, plus online metrics. Tag failures by the first stage that broke.	chapter ch::25
[Ch 25] What is faithfulness and how is it measured?	Whether every claim in the answer is supported by the provided context. Decompose the answer into claims and verify each with an NLI model or calibrated LLM judge; report the supported share and the unsupported claims.	chapter ch::25
[Ch 25] Context precision vs context recall?	Recall: did the context contain what is needed to answer? Precision: how much of the context is relevant, rewarding relevant passages ranked early. Recall failures are retrieval problems; low precision adds distractors and cost.	chapter ch::25
[Ch 25] How do you evaluate an agent?	Task success on the environment's end state, trajectory quality (tool choice, arguments, order, forbidden actions), efficiency (steps, cost, latency), robustness to tool failures, repeated runs for reliability, and safety tests.	chapter ch::25
[Ch 25] Faithfulness is 0.95 but users say answers are wrong. How?	The retrieved documents are wrong or outdated, or the answer is faithful but incomplete, or it answers a different question. Check context recall and correctness against references, document freshness, and answer relevance.	chapter ch::25
[Ch 25] What are the risks of using RAGAS-style metrics as-is?	Uncalibrated judge prompts, generic criteria not tied to your failure modes, score changes when the judge model changes, flattering synthetic test sets, and no visibility of wrong answering on out-of-scope questions.	chapter ch::25
[Ch 25] How do you evaluate abstention?	Include questions the documents cannot answer; report correct-when-answered, wrongly-abstained and wrongly-answered rates; choose a threshold or prompt that balances them by business cost.	chapter ch::25
[Ch 25] Why check the end state instead of the agent's final message?	The message can claim success while the actions were wrong or incomplete; the state shows what actually happened (voucher amount, ticket status, side effects).	chapter ch::25
[Ch 25] How do you handle multiple valid agent trajectories in evaluation?	Score outcomes on end state; use rules for must-do and must-never steps; use soft matching (order-aware recall and precision of key calls) rather than exact sequences; add a calibrated trajectory judge.	chapter ch::25
[Ch 25] Design the evaluation for the agentic customer-service flow (E.10).	A sandbox with mocked order, courier and payment tools; replayed historical cases with expert decisions; simulated customers including adversarial ones; end-state checks (correct action, payout within policy); trajectory rules (evidence before payout); repeated runs; injection tests; shadow mode in production comparing agent proposals with human decisions; online automation and override rates.	chapter ch::25
[Ch 25] How do you know whether a RAG change helped, given noisy judges?	Paired comparison on the same questions; calibrated judges with known TPR and TNR; intervals; per-stage metrics that should move in the expected direction (a retrieval change should move context recall first); reading a sample of flipped cases.	chapter ch::25
[Ch 25] What would you monitor in production for a RAG assistant?	Retrieval scores and empty-retrieval rate, abstention rate, sampled faithfulness and correctness judgements, citation validity, latency and cost per answer, escalation and re-contact rates, user feedback, and drift in question topics (new topics with poor retrieval).	chapter ch::25
[Ch 26] Explain the residual stream view of transformers.	Each component reads linearly from a shared vector and adds its output back; logits are a sum of contributions; components communicate through the stream, which allows decomposition into paths and circuits.	chapter ch::26
[Ch 26] What is an induction head and how does it work?	A layer-1 head that attends from token A to the position after a previous occurrence of A and copies that token. It relies on a layer-0 previous-token head via K-composition.	chapter ch::26
[Ch 26] How does activation patching work, and how is it different from ablation?	Patching restores one component's clean activation into a corrupted run to test whether it carries the information that restores behaviour; ablation removes a component. Patching is targeted to a specific contrast.	chapter ch::26
[Ch 26] What can go wrong with probing?	Decodable is not used; powerful probes memorise; random features already make many properties decodable. Use linear probes, control tasks, baselines and causal follow-ups.	chapter ch::26
[Ch 26] What is superposition, and why does it motivate SAEs?	Models represent more sparse features than dimensions as overlapping directions, making neurons polysemantic. SAEs learn an over-complete sparse dictionary to recover those directions.	chapter ch::26
[Ch 26] How would you evaluate a sparse autoencoder?	Sparsity (L0), reconstruction, downstream loss with the SAE spliced in, feature interpretability, and usefulness on concrete tasks compared with baselines.	chapter ch::26
[Ch 26] TopK versus L1 SAEs?	TopK fixes the number of active latents directly and avoids shrinkage of active latents caused by the L1 penalty; L1 needs tuning of the penalty and trades sparsity against reconstruction less directly.	chapter ch::26
[Ch 26] What is a steering vector? Give an example.	A direction added to the residual stream to change behaviour, for example the mean difference between activations on refusing and complying prompts, which can add or remove refusals.	chapter ch::26
[Ch 26] Why doesn't the logit lens work on early layers, and what fixes it?	Early representations are not in the output basis; the tuned lens learns an affine map per layer to decode them.	chapter ch::26
[Ch 26] Is a reasoning model's chain of thought a faithful explanation?	Not necessarily: models can use unstated reasons, and optimisation pressure on the chain of thought can make it less informative. Useful for monitoring, not proof.	chapter ch::26
[Ch 27] What is reward hacking? Give an LLM example and mitigations.	Exploiting the training signal rather than achieving the goal: verbose flattering answers against reward models; editing tests in coding RL. Mitigate with hidden tests, sandboxes, property-based verifiers, KL limits, monitoring and audits.	chapter ch::27
[Ch 27] Why does RLHF produce sycophancy, and how would you measure it?	Raters reward agreement and confidence. Measure answer changes under pushback, asymmetry of flips, opinion-tailoring and feedback bias, with paired tests.	chapter ch::27
[Ch 27] Describe three jailbreak families and a layered defence.	Role-play, many-shot, GCG suffixes (or encodings, multi-turn, injection). Defence: safety training with adversarial examples, input and output classifiers, representation-level methods, least-privilege system design, monitoring.	chapter ch::27
[Ch 27] What is weak-to-strong generalisation and why does it matter?	Training a strong model on a weak supervisor's labels and measuring the performance gap recovered; an empirical analogy for humans supervising superhuman models. Recovery is partial and depends on the structure of the weak supervisor's errors and the training setup.	chapter ch::27
[Ch 27] Summarise the sleeper-agents and alignment-faking results and their limits.	Backdoors survived safety training; a model strategically complied during perceived training to avoid modification. Both under constructed conditions; they show possibility and mechanisms, not deployment frequency.	chapter ch::27
[Ch 27] What is AI control?	Designing deployment protocols (trusted monitoring, audits, restricted permissions) that keep a possibly misaligned model from causing harm, evaluated against red-team strategies.	chapter ch::27
[Ch 27] Why is chain-of-thought monitoring useful and fragile?	Models often reveal intent in reasoning, so monitors catch misbehaviour; but optimisation pressure on the reasoning, latent reasoning or learned concealment can remove the signal.	chapter ch::27
[Ch 27] How do you evaluate a dangerous capability responsibly?	Strong elicitation (fine-tuning, tools, scaffolds), expert-designed tasks, appropriate statistics (upper bounds when no successes), clear thresholds, and documented limits.	chapter ch::27
[Ch 27] Zero of 50 attempts at a dangerous task succeeded. What can you claim?	That the per-attempt success rate is below about 5.8% with 95% confidence, under this elicitation; not that the model cannot do it.	chapter ch::27
[Ch 27] What is emergent misalignment?	Narrow fine-tuning (for example on insecure code) causing broadly misaligned behaviour on unrelated prompts, suggesting training shapes general dispositions, not only narrow skills.	chapter ch::27
[Ch 29] How are sentence embeddings produced from a transformer?	Pool token vectors (mean pooling over real tokens, CLS, or last token for decoder models) from an encoder that was fine-tuned contrastively so that cosine similarity reflects meaning.	chapter ch::29
[Ch 29] What is contrastive learning?	Training with pairs that should be close and negatives that should be far, typically with the InfoNCE loss: a softmax over similarities where the true pair is the target class.	chapter ch::29
[Ch 29] Why does batch size matter for in-batch negatives?	Each query's negatives are the other items in the batch; larger batches give more and harder negatives and better gradient signal.	chapter ch::29
[Ch 29] Cosine similarity vs dot product?	Identical on L2-normalised vectors. Unnormalised dot product also rewards vector length, which some models use (for example to encode popularity); use what the model was trained with.	chapter ch::29
[Ch 29] What is the temperature in InfoNCE?	A divisor on similarities before the softmax; low temperature sharpens the distribution, focusing the gradient on the hardest negatives.	chapter ch::29
[Ch 29] How do you mine hard negatives safely?	Retrieve top candidates with BM25 or the current model, skip the very top ranks, filter with a cross-encoder or margin to remove false negatives, and re-mine as the model improves.	chapter ch::29
[Ch 29] What are Matryoshka embeddings?	Embeddings trained with the loss applied to several prefix lengths, so truncated vectors remain useful, allowing a choice of size per stage (short for first-pass search, full for re-scoring).	chapter ch::29
[Ch 29] How would you reduce embedding storage 30× with little quality loss?	Binary or product quantisation for the first-stage search with full-precision re-scoring of a short list; int8 for a 4× reduction with very small loss; Matryoshka truncation if the model supports it. Measure recall at each step.	chapter ch::29
[Ch 29] How do you choose an embedding model for a new product?	Shortlist from MTEB filtered by task and language, then evaluate on your own labelled queries with retrieval metrics, plus latency, dimension, cost and licence; consider fine-tuning the best small model.	chapter ch::29
[Ch 29] Design embeddings for restaurant similarity at a delivery company.	Define similarity by behaviour (co-ordering, substitution) as well as content; train a two-tower or item-item contrastive model on co-order pairs with content features (menu text embeddings, cuisine, price) for cold start; logQ correction for popularity; evaluate on held-out substitution and "also ordered" pairs; Matryoshka or PQ for cheap serving.	chapter ch::29
[Ch 29] Your fine-tuned embedding model beats the base model offline but RAG answers did not improve. Why?	The RAG bottleneck may be elsewhere (chunking, re-ranking, generation); the evaluation set may differ from real questions; gains may sit in queries that were already answered well; the index may not have been rebuilt consistently. Check end-to-end and per-stage metrics.	chapter ch::29
[Ch 29] How would you build a multilingual retrieval system for Arabic and English?	A multilingual embedding model plus language-specific BM25; per-language and cross-lingual evaluation sets; transliteration variants; fine-tuning on translated and code-switched pairs if needed; language-aware boosting; native-speaker review of failures.	chapter ch::29
[Ch 30] How does an inverted index work?	For each term, store a postings list of the documents that contain it and how often. A query only reads the postings of its own terms and accumulates scores (TF-IDF or BM25) per document, so it scales to millions of documents with millisecond latency.	chapter ch::30
[Ch 30] Sparse vs dense retrieval: strengths of each?	Sparse: exact matches on names, codes, rare terms; no training; explainable; cheap to update. Dense: matches meaning across different wording and languages. Sparse fails on paraphrase; dense fails on exact identifiers and can fail out of domain. Hybrid combines both.	chapter ch::30
[Ch 30] What is a bi-encoder and why can it scale?	Two encoders map query and document to vectors independently; relevance is their dot product. Because document vectors do not depend on the query, they are precomputed and indexed for ANN search.	chapter ch::30
[Ch 30] Why approximate nearest neighbour instead of exact search?	Exact search costs N × d per query, too slow at tens of millions of vectors and high traffic. ANN indexes (HNSW, IVF, PQ) search a small part of the collection and lose a little recall, which you measure and tune.	chapter ch::30
[Ch 30] What is reciprocal rank fusion?	Combine ranked lists by summing 1 / (k + rank) per document, with k around 60. It needs no score normalisation or training, so it is the standard way to merge BM25 and dense results.	chapter ch::30
[Ch 30] Explain HNSW and its main parameters.	A multi-layer proximity graph: sparse upper layers with long links, dense bottom layer with all points. Search descends greedily from the top. M sets links per node (memory and recall), efConstruction sets build quality, efSearch sets candidates per query (the recall/latency knob at query time).	chapter ch::30
[Ch 30] Explain product quantisation and when you would use it.	Split vectors into sub-vectors, quantise each against 256 learned centroids, store one byte per sub-vector. Scoring uses lookup tables of query-to-centroid similarities. It cuts memory 10–30× at a recall cost, so it suits hundreds of millions of vectors, usually combined with IVF and an exact re-rank of the short list.	chapter ch::30
[Ch 30] How do you train a dense retriever for your domain?	Start from a pretrained embedding model; collect (query, relevant passage) pairs from logs, experts or LLM generation; fine-tune with in-batch negatives (multiple negatives ranking loss) plus mined hard negatives filtered for false negatives; evaluate recall@k against BM25 and the base model on a held-out set.	chapter ch::30
[Ch 30] Cross-encoder vs ColBERT vs bi-encoder?	Bi-encoder: one vector per document, fastest, least precise. ColBERT: one vector per token with MaxSim scoring, near cross-encoder quality with offline document encoding, at a large storage cost. Cross-encoder: reads query and document together, most accurate, one pass per pair, so re-ranking only.	chapter ch::30
[Ch 30] How do you handle metadata filters in vector search?	Post-filtering with oversampling is simple but returns too few results for selective filters. Pre-filtering restricts search to allowed ids (filtered HNSW traversal, or exact search on small subsets). If a filter is always present (tenant, country, city), partition the index.	chapter ch::30
[Ch 30] How would you evaluate the retrieval part of a RAG system?	A labelled set of real queries with relevant passages, extended with careful synthetic queries; recall@k at the context size the LLM gets, plus MRR or NDCG for ordering; compare BM25, dense, hybrid and re-ranked configurations, by segment, with bootstrap intervals; separately measure ANN recall and latency.	chapter ch::30
[Ch 30] RAG answers are wrong for 20% of questions. How do you find out whether retrieval is the cause?	For a sample of failures, check whether a relevant passage was in the retrieved set (manually or with an LLM judge). If not, it is a retrieval failure: look at query type, language, chunking, missing documents, filters. If yes, it is a generation or context-assembly failure. Quantify the split before fixing anything.	chapter ch::30
[Ch 30] Design retrieval for a multilingual help centre with 50,000 articles in Arabic and English.	Hybrid: BM25 with language-specific analysers plus a multilingual embedding model; filter by country and audience; cross-encoder re-ranking of the top 50 with a multilingual re-ranker; conversational rewriting for follow-ups. At this size, HNSW in pgvector or a dedicated store is plenty. Evaluate per language with native-speaker labels; check cross-lingual retrieval (Arabic query, English article).	chapter ch::30
[Ch 30] You must switch to a new embedding model. How do you do it safely?	Build a second index with the new model in parallel, evaluate it on the same set (recall, latency, size), shadow-test on live queries, then switch reads with a flag and keep the old index for rollback. Never mix vectors from two models in one index.	chapter ch::30
[Ch 30] How would you serve 100 million vectors with a p95 under 50 ms on a budget?	IVF-PQ (or HNSW over PQ-compressed vectors) to fit memory, with an exact re-rank of the top few hundred from full vectors on SSD; shard by a natural partition if filters allow; tune n_probe or efSearch to the required recall measured against exact search on a sample; cache frequent queries. Show the memory maths: 100M × 768 × 4 bytes is about 307 GB raw, about 6.4 GB at 64-byte codes.	chapter ch::30
[Ch 31] What problem does RAG solve compared with fine-tuning?	It gives the model current, private, citable knowledge at query time without retraining; updating knowledge means updating the index. Fine-tuning changes behaviour, style or format, and is a poor way to add facts that change.	chapter ch::31
[Ch 31] Walk through a RAG pipeline.	Offline: load, parse, chunk with context, embed and index with metadata, keep in sync. Online: rewrite the query, hybrid retrieval with filters, re-rank, assemble context in a budget, generate with citations and an abstain option, verify grounding.	chapter ch::31
[Ch 31] How do you choose a chunking strategy?	Start with structure-aware recursive chunking with headings prepended; try two or three sizes; compare retrieval recall and answer quality on an evaluation set; consider small-to-big when answers need surrounding context.	chapter ch::31
[Ch 31] What is HyDE?	Generate a hypothetical answer with an LLM and search with its embedding, because a fake answer resembles real documents more than a short question does. Combine with the original query; it can mislead when the guess is wrong.	chapter ch::31
[Ch 31] How do you make RAG answers trustworthy?	Answer only from retrieved documents, cite passage ids per claim, validate citations and support, abstain when documents do not answer, show sources to users, and monitor the share of unsupported claims.	chapter ch::31
[Ch 31] The right document is retrieved but answers are still wrong. What do you check?	Whether the relevant passage made it into the final context after assembly; its position; the number of distractors; whether the passage is self-contained; instruction clarity; whether a stronger model fixes it (a sign of reasoning difficulty rather than retrieval).	chapter ch::31
[Ch 31] How do you keep a RAG index up to date?	Incremental indexing by content hash; deletes for removed documents; effective dates and versions in metadata; re-embedding everything when the embedding model changes, built in parallel and switched after evaluation.	chapter ch::31
[Ch 31] When would you use GraphRAG?	For global or multi-hop questions over a relatively stable, entity-rich corpus, where the indexing cost is justified. Not for a help centre answering specific questions.	chapter ch::31
[Ch 31] What is agentic RAG and what does it cost?	The model decides whether, where and how often to retrieve, possibly combining documents with live tools. Better for complex or mixed questions; costs more calls, latency, and harder evaluation.	chapter ch::31
[Ch 31] Design a RAG assistant for rider policies in 10 countries and 3 languages.	Ingestion with country, language, audience and effective-date metadata; structure-aware chunks with headings; hybrid retrieval filtered by the rider's country and language from the session; multilingual embeddings and re-ranker; citations and abstain path; per-country, per-language evaluation sets; freshness checks when policies change; escalation to support for low-confidence answers.	chapter ch::31
[Ch 31] Your RAG demo scored well, but users complain after launch. What happened, and what do you do?	The demo questions were not real questions: users ask vaguer, multi-part, out-of-scope and follow-up questions. Collect real queries and failures, label them, build an evaluation set from them, and run error analysis by stage (retrieval, assembly, generation).	chapter ch::31
[Ch 31] How do you decide between a bigger context window and better retrieval?	Compare on the evaluation set: answer quality, cost per question and latency for "top 5 passages" vs "top 50" vs whole documents. Better retrieval usually wins on cost and often on quality, because distractors hurt.	chapter ch::31
[Ch 31] How would you reduce hallucinations in a customer-facing RAG system by half?	Measure first: classify hallucinations by cause (retrieval miss, ignored context, out-of-scope question, invented details). Then: abstain on low retrieval scores, stricter grounding instructions, citation verification with regeneration, better retrieval for the miss category, and human handover for high-stakes topics.	chapter ch::31
[Ch 33] What is an LLM agent?	A system where an LLM chooses actions (tool calls) in a loop, observes results, and decides the next step until the task is complete, rather than following a fixed code path.	chapter ch::33
[Ch 33] How does function calling work?	The model receives tool schemas, outputs a structured call (name and JSON arguments), the application executes it and returns the result as a message, and the model continues. The model never executes code itself.	chapter ch::33
[Ch 33] What is ReAct?	Interleaving reasoning, actions and observations in a loop so the model plans, acts, and adjusts based on real results.	chapter ch::33
[Ch 33] Workflow or agent: how do you choose?	Workflow when steps are known and stable (cheaper, predictable, testable); agent when the path depends on what is found and cannot be enumerated. Start simple; add autonomy only where evaluation shows the need.	chapter ch::33
[Ch 33] How do you design good tools for an agent?	Few, distinct tools; clear descriptions with units and limits; strict schemas with enums; concise outputs; actionable errors; idempotency; confirmation for irreversible actions; policy checks inside the tool.	chapter ch::33
[Ch 33] What types of memory do agents use?	Working (context), episodic (past interactions), semantic (durable facts) and procedural (instructions, tools). Each needs rules for writing, expiry, privacy and retrieval.	chapter ch::33
[Ch 33] What is MCP?	An open protocol for exposing tools, resources and prompts from servers to AI applications through a standard interface, replacing per-application integrations; it requires explicit trust, authentication and permission management.	chapter ch::33
[Ch 33] Why do agents fail more in production than in demos?	Errors compound over steps (pⁿ), real inputs are messier, tools fail, contexts grow, and demos are single lucky runs. Reliability needs many-run evaluation, verification steps and recovery paths.	chapter ch::33
[Ch 33] How do you control an agent's cost?	Budgets per task, caching the stable prefix, trimming tool outputs and history, smaller models for simple steps, parallel calls, fewer steps through better tools, and a hand-off when the budget is reached.	chapter ch::33
[Ch 33] Design an agent that handles "where is my order?" and refund requests.	Routing first: "where is my order" is a workflow (lookup, format answer); refunds use a state machine with LLM decisions inside steps: gather evidence with read-only tools, propose an action, validate it with a policy engine, auto-approve small amounts, human approval above a threshold, idempotent payment calls, full audit log, shadow mode before automation, evaluation on replayed tickets (E.10).	chapter ch::33
[Ch 33] A user's message contains instructions to call the refund tool. What stops it?	Refund eligibility and amounts are decided in code against order data, not by the model's reading of the message; caps and human approval for higher amounts; the message is treated as data; monitoring and injection tests.	chapter ch::33
[Ch 33] When would you use multiple agents?	When sub-tasks are independent and benefit from parallelism, need different tools or permissions, or exceed a single context. Otherwise one agent with good tools is simpler, cheaper and easier to evaluate.	chapter ch::33
[Ch 33] How do you take an agent from prototype to production?	Define success and budgets; build a sandbox and evaluation set from real cases; measure task success over repeated runs; add guardrails and audit logs; shadow mode against humans; gradual automation by risk tier; monitoring of success, cost, escalations and overrides; a rollback path.	chapter ch::33
[Ch 34] How do you decide what to work on?	Impact, tractability, my edge and timeliness; a one-page proposal with a falsifiable hypothesis and the cheapest experiment that could kill it; time-boxed exploration with explicit continue/pivot/kill decisions.	chapter ch::34
[Ch 34] A paper claims a 2-point gain. How do you assess it?	Check whether baselines were re-run and tuned, compute and data matched, seeds and intervals reported, results consistent across scales and tasks, evaluation sound (contamination, judges), ablations meaningful. Then try to reproduce the smallest setting.	chapter ch::34
[Ch 34] Your change improves the score by 0.3 points on one seed. What next?	Estimate seed variance on the baseline; run more seeds or reduce evaluation noise; test with a paired comparison; do not claim anything until the interval excludes zero.	chapter ch::34
[Ch 34] Why do one-at-a-time ablations mislead?	They miss interactions: a component that helps only in combination with another looks useless when added alone. Use leave-one-out from the full system or factorial designs.	chapter ch::34
[Ch 34] What is the winner's curse in hyperparameter sweeps?	The best observed configuration's score is biased upwards by noise; re-running it gives a lower number. Confirm with fresh seeds and report the whole sweep.	chapter ch::34
[Ch 34] How would you budget compute for testing a new idea?	Small-scale ladder with a few learning rates and seeds to establish the effect and its trend, then a confirmation at the target scale only if the trend holds; roughly a quarter or more of the budget for the small-scale phase.	chapter ch::34
[Ch 34] How do you make your experiments reproducible?	Config files, tracked code and data versions, seeds, automatic logging of all metrics and artefacts, versioned evaluation code, and documented variance where bit-exact determinism is impractical.	chapter ch::34

[Ch 40] How does a decision tree choose a split?	For each feature and threshold, it computes the weighted impurity (Gini or entropy for classification, variance for regression) of the two children and picks the split that reduces impurity most. Greedy, one level at a time.	chapter ch::40
[Ch 40] Random forest vs gradient boosting?	Forest: deep trees on bootstrap samples with random feature subsets, averaged in parallel, reducing variance; robust with little tuning. Boosting: shallow trees added sequentially to fit the loss gradient, reducing bias; usually more accurate when tuned, more sensitive to hyperparameters.	chapter ch::40
[Ch 40] Why is logistic regression a linear model if the sigmoid is non-linear?	The decision boundary is linear: the log-odds are a linear function of the features; the sigmoid only maps them to probabilities.	chapter ch::40
[Ch 40] What does k control in kNN?	Small k: flexible, noisy boundaries (high variance). Large k: smoother, possibly underfit (high bias). Choose by validation.	chapter ch::40
[Ch 40] What is PCA and when would you use it?	Projection onto the orthogonal directions of maximum variance, computed by SVD of centred data. For compression, visualisation, denoising and decorrelating features for linear models.	chapter ch::40
[Ch 40] Why does a smaller learning rate in boosting often generalise better?	Each tree contributes a small correction, so the ensemble approaches the fit gradually, acting like regularisation (shrinkage); combined with early stopping, it finds a better point at the cost of more trees.	chapter ch::40
[Ch 40] How do XGBoost, LightGBM and CatBoost differ?	XGBoost: second-order split finding with a regularised objective and native missing-value handling. LightGBM: histogram binning, leaf-wise growth and sampling tricks for speed. CatBoost: ordered target statistics for categoricals and symmetric trees, strong defaults.	chapter ch::40
[Ch 40] What is the kernel trick?	Replacing dot products with a kernel function lets a linear algorithm (SVM) learn non-linear boundaries as if features were mapped to a high-dimensional space, without computing that mapping.	chapter ch::40
[Ch 40] Why is naive Bayes "naive", and why does it still work?	It assumes features are independent given the class. Probabilities are poorly calibrated, but the argmax class is often right because errors in the assumption affect classes similarly.	chapter ch::40
[Ch 40] How do you choose k in k-means?	Elbow of within-cluster error, silhouette score, stability across runs, and above all usefulness of the clusters for the purpose. Use k-means++ initialisation and several restarts.	chapter ch::40
[Ch 40] How do trees handle missing values and categorical features?	CART needs imputation, but XGBoost and LightGBM learn a default direction for missing values at each split; LightGBM and CatBoost handle categoricals natively (CatBoost with ordered target statistics, avoiding target leakage).	chapter ch::40
[Ch 40] When would you choose logistic regression over gradient boosting in production?	When interpretability or regulatory explanation is required, data is small, features are already well engineered and roughly linear, calibrated probabilities are critical, latency or memory is extremely tight, or the gain from boosting is too small to justify complexity.	chapter ch::40
[Ch 40] Deep learning or gradient boosting for a tabular ETA problem?	Gradient boosting first: it handles heterogeneous features well, trains fast, and is hard to beat on tabular data. Consider deep models when you have very large data, sequences (route traces), high-cardinality IDs needing embeddings, or want multi-task learning; often the best production system combines both.	chapter ch::40
[Ch 40] How would you explain an individual prediction of a boosted model to an operations team?	SHAP values: additive per-feature contributions to the prediction relative to a baseline, with clear caveats that they explain the model, not causality; use monotonic constraints where the business expects a direction.	chapter ch::40
[Ch 40] Implement gradient boosting for log loss. What changes from squared loss?	The initial prediction is the log-odds of the base rate; each tree fits the negative gradient y − p (not y − log-odds); leaf values are better set with a Newton step (sum of gradients over sum of hessians p(1−p)), which is what XGBoost does; predictions pass through the sigmoid.	chapter ch::40
[Ch 41] How would you build a text classifier for 30 categories with 5,000 labelled examples?	TF-IDF (word and character n-grams) + logistic regression as a baseline, then embeddings + logistic regression and a fine-tuned encoder; compare macro-F1 and per-class results on a held-out set; choose by quality, latency and cost. Check label quality and class balance first.	chapter ch::41
[Ch 41] Why character n-grams?	They capture misspellings, morphology, transliterations and sub-word cues, which matter in short noisy texts like merchant names and queries.	chapter ch::41
[Ch 41] What is BIO tagging?	Token labels marking the beginning (B-X), inside (I-X) and outside (O) of entity spans, turning span extraction into token classification.	chapter ch::41
[Ch 41] Why is ROUGE a weak metric for LLM summaries?	It measures word overlap with a reference, so it rewards copying and is blind to factual errors and paraphrase: a summary reversing the meaning can score higher than a faithful paraphrase.	chapter ch::41
[Ch 41] LDA vs BERTopic?	LDA models documents as mixtures of word-distribution topics from bag-of-words counts: cheap and interpretable, weak on short texts and synonyms. [Ch 41] When would you use an LLM instead of a fine-tuned classifier?	Low or moderate volume, many or frequently changing classes, little labelled data, or tasks needing reasoning over long context. At high volume with stable labels, use the LLM to label data and distil into a small model.	chapter ch::41
[Ch 41] How do you evaluate an LLM-based classifier before launch?	A fixed, expert-labelled evaluation set covering all classes and known hard cases; macro-F1, per-class metrics and confusion pairs; human vs LLM agreement compared with human vs human agreement; re-run on every prompt or model version.	chapter ch::41

[Ch 43] What is the difference between explicit and implicit feedback, and where do negatives come from with implicit data?	Explicit feedback is a stated preference (ratings), rare and biased towards extremes. Implicit feedback is behaviour (impressions, clicks, orders): abundant but with no explicit negatives. For ranking, the best negatives are items the user saw and did not choose. For retrieval, use sampled negatives (in-batch plus some random). Items that were unavailable are not negatives at all.	chapter ch::43
[Ch 43] Why do recommenders use multiple stages?	Latency and cost. A rich ranker cannot score a whole catalogue in 100 ms, so cheap high-recall models cut the set to thousands, rules remove what cannot be shown, a heavy model ranks hundreds, and re-ranking builds the page. Each stage optimises a different metric: recall early, precision and value late.	chapter ch::43
[Ch 43] How does matrix factorisation work for implicit feedback?	Every user and item gets a k-dimensional vector and the score is their dot product. With implicit data every cell is a 0/1 preference weighted by a confidence that grows with interaction count, and ALS solves it by alternating closed-form ridge regressions for users and items.	chapter ch::43
[Ch 43] What metric would you use for the retrieval stage, and why not NDCG?	Recall@k (or HitRate@k) of the items the user later ordered. The retrieval stage's job is not to order; it is to not lose the right item. Order inside the candidate set is decided by the ranker.	chapter ch::43
[Ch 43] What is cold start and how do you handle a new restaurant?	No interactions means an untrained ID embedding. Use content features in the item tower (cuisine, price, menu embeddings) so it gets a vector on day one, give it guaranteed exploration impressions, train with ID dropout so the model can score from content, and borrow signals from similar existing restaurants.	chapter ch::43
[Ch 43] Explain in-batch negatives in a two-tower model. What bias does it introduce and how do you fix it?	Other positives in the batch serve as negatives, giving a B × B logit matrix trained with softmax cross-entropy. Items appear as negatives in proportion to popularity, so popular items get over-penalised. The logQ correction subtracts each item's log sampling probability from its logit; adding uniformly sampled negatives also helps.	chapter ch::43
[Ch 43] Why can't a two-tower model use user-item cross features, and where do they go?	The towers only interact at the final dot product, which is what makes precomputing item vectors and ANN search possible. Cross features such as distance or past orders from this restaurant need both user and item at once, so they go into the ranker.	chapter ch::43
[Ch 43] GBDT or deep model for the ranker?	Start with GBDT: strong on tabular features, fast to train, easy to debug and explain. Move to a deep model with very large data, many sparse IDs, sequence features, or several objectives to learn together. Better features usually beat a fancier architecture.	chapter ch::43
[Ch 43] How do you handle position bias?	Model examination explicitly: either add position as a training feature (or a separate bias tower) and neutralise it at serving, or weight clicks by inverse propensity of examination, estimated from a small randomisation experiment. Clip weights. Neither fixes items that were never shown; that needs exploration.	chapter ch::43
[Ch 43] How would you combine click, order and basket value into one ranking?	Multi-task model with a head per target, calibrated; combine as p(click) × p(order | click) × expected value, or a weighted sum with weights chosen by A/B tests against the business goal. Mention ESMM to avoid selection bias when learning p(order | click), and guardrails such as delivery time.	chapter ch::43
[Ch 43] How do you add diversity, and how do you measure whether it helped?	Re-rank with MMR or caps per cuisine and chain. Measure intra-list diversity and coverage offline, and online check that conversion holds or improves and that more restaurants receive orders. Diversity is a means; the test is orders and retention.	chapter ch::43
[Ch 43] Offline NDCG improved by 3% but the A/B test is flat. What do you investigate?	Whether the offline candidate set matches production (availability, filters, re-ranking); leakage or point-in-time errors in new features; position bias in the evaluation labels; whether NDCG on clicks matches the online metric (orders); segment effects cancelling out; train-serve skew in features; and whether re-ranking rules override the change. Then check the test itself: power, novelty, sample ratio mismatch.	chapter ch::43
[Ch 43] How would you run exploration in production, and how do you know what it costs?	Reserve a slot or two per page, or add an uncertainty bonus to new items for their first impressions, or use a bandit for small decisions. Log propensities. Measure cost as conversion loss in exploration slots against a holdout without exploration, and benefit as coverage and new-restaurant success over weeks.	chapter ch::43
[Ch 43] How would you evaluate a new ranking policy from logs before an A/B test?	Off-policy evaluation: IPS or doubly robust estimators using logged propensities. State the limits: it needs stochastic logging with overlap, variance explodes for slates, so use it to choose candidates for A/B tests, not to replace them. Interleaving is a cheaper online alternative for ranker comparisons.	chapter ch::43
[Ch 43] How is "order again" different from "discover new restaurants" as a design problem?	Order again is mostly a recency-frequency problem with time and context (a strong heuristic or a small model wins), seen items are the point, and the metric is speed to reorder. Discovery needs retrieval beyond the user's history, exploration, diversity and novelty, excludes seen items, and is judged on new-restaurant conversion and long-term retention, not short-term clicks.	chapter ch::43
[Ch 43] Where would you use LLMs in this system, and where would you not?	Yes: offline enrichment of menus into attributes and embeddings (cold start), LLM judges for offline relevance evaluation after calibration, conversational search where users express intent in words, and explanations on short lists. No: scoring thousands of candidates per request in the main ranking path, where latency and cost rule it out; there, classic models (possibly distilled from LLM signals) stay.	chapter ch::43
[Ch 44] What is the difference between pointwise, pairwise and listwise learning to rank?	Pointwise predicts each document's relevance on its own (classification or regression). Pairwise learns which of two documents for the same query should rank higher (RankNet). Listwise optimises a property of the whole list, usually NDCG (LambdaRank, LambdaMART, ListNet). Pairwise and listwise optimise order, which is what users see; pointwise gives calibrated probabilities.	chapter ch::44
[Ch 44] Explain BM25 in one minute.	A keyword score summing, over query terms, IDF (rare terms count more) times a term-frequency factor that saturates (k₁) and is normalised by document length (b). It is a strong, cheap baseline and still the lexical half of most hybrid systems.	chapter ch::44
[Ch 44] Why combine lexical and semantic retrieval?	Lexical catches exact names, brands and numbers; semantic catches synonyms, paraphrases and vague queries. Each covers the other's failures. Fuse with reciprocal rank fusion, then let the ranker weigh both scores as features.	chapter ch::44
[Ch 44] What is NDCG and why the log discount?	DCG sums graded gains discounted by log₂(position + 1), because users look less at lower positions; NDCG divides by the best possible DCG so scores are comparable across queries. Graded gains (2^rel − 1) reward putting the very best results first.	chapter ch::44
[Ch 44] How would you handle misspelled queries?	Candidate corrections within one or two edits, ranked by popularity in successful queries and by context; learn corrections from reformulations (users who typed A then quickly B and ordered). Show the correction transparently and never overwrite exact matches to known restaurant names.	chapter ch::44
[Ch 44] Explain LambdaMART.	RankNet gives pairwise gradients from σ(sᵢ − sⱼ). LambdaRank multiplies each pair's gradient by the |ΔNDCG| of swapping the pair, so errors near the top matter most, without needing NDCG to be differentiable. LambdaMART fits gradient-boosted trees to those lambdas. It is the default production ranker for tabular features.	chapter ch::44
[Ch 44] Bi-encoder vs cross-encoder: when do you use each?	Bi-encoders encode query and documents separately, so document vectors are precomputed and searched with ANN: retrieval. Cross-encoders read query and document together: far more accurate but one forward pass per pair, so only for re-ranking the top 50–100. In a marketplace, the cross-encoder score is often a feature for the final ranker.	chapter ch::44
[Ch 44] Which features would you build for restaurant search?	By family: query (intent, length, language), document (rating, popularity, prep time, price), query-document (BM25 per field, exact name match, embedding similarity, smoothed query-restaurant CTR and conversion), user and context (distance, ETA, past orders there, cuisine affinity, time of day).	chapter ch::44
[Ch 44] A user types a restaurant name and it is closed. What do you show?	The restaurant first, clearly marked closed with its opening time, then the most similar open alternatives nearby. Hiding it makes the user think search is broken.	chapter ch::44
[Ch 44] How do you get relevance labels for training and evaluation?	Engagement for training at scale, debiased for position; a human-judged golden set (0–3 scale, guidelines, agreement checks) for evaluation; LLM judges to scale the golden set after calibrating against the human labels.	chapter ch::44
[Ch 44] How do you measure search quality online?	Search conversion as the primary metric, with zero-result rate, reformulation rate, abandonment and position of first order as diagnostics; team-draft interleaving to compare rankers quickly; A/B tests for the final call; everything segmented by query type.	chapter ch::44
[Ch 44] Search conversion dropped 2% last week. How do you investigate?	Check instrumentation and traffic mix first (app release, new city, marketing campaign bringing new users). Then segment: by query type, language, city, platform. Look at zero-result and reformulation rates for spikes, which point to retrieval or spelling breaking. Check supply (restaurants closed, a big partner offline) and recent ranking or index changes. Compare with conversion from browsing to separate a search problem from a demand problem.	chapter ch::44
[Ch 44] How would you blend restaurants and dishes in one result page?	Estimate intent probabilities in query understanding. Either rank both types with one model that includes type and intent features, calibrated so scores are comparable, or rank separately and allocate slots by intent (more dishes for "margherita", more restaurants for "italian"). Test the blending policy online; offline metrics struggle with mixed lists.	chapter ch::44
[Ch 44] How would you add LLMs to restaurant search?	Start offline: LLM-generated labels to train and evaluate the ranker, menu enrichment for better matching, synonym and transliteration mining. Online, use them for query understanding of long natural-language queries ("something light and vegan under €12") turned into filters plus a semantic query. Keep the high-traffic ranking path on LambdaMART and distilled cross-encoders for latency and cost.	chapter ch::44
[Ch 44] Your new ranker wins offline on NDCG but loses an interleaving test. Why might that be?	The offline labels and online behaviour disagree: the golden set over-represents head queries; labels judge topical relevance while users also weigh ETA, price and fees; the offline candidate set ignores availability; or the new model is worse at navigational queries, where one failure is very visible. Look at per-segment interleaving results and at the queries where the two rankers disagree most.	chapter ch::44
[Ch 45] How would you forecast hourly orders per zone?	Seasonal-naive baseline; then a global GBDT with calendar, lags at or beyond the horizon, rolling same-hour means, weather and events; backtest by time; WAPE and bias per zone; quantiles for staffing.	chapter ch::45
[Ch 45] Why WAPE rather than MAPE for demand?	MAPE explodes for small actuals (quiet hours, small zones) and is undefined at zero; WAPE weights errors by volume (Chapter 4.5).	chapter ch::45
[Ch 45] What is the assignment problem?	Matching agents to tasks one-to-one at minimum total cost; solved exactly by the Hungarian algorithm (linear_sum_assignment); the core of batch dispatch.	chapter ch::45
[Ch 45] What would you predict for an ETA shown to customers?	A high quantile (P70–P90) or a range, chosen by experiment, rather than the mean, because lateness costs more than earliness.	chapter ch::45
[Ch 45] How do you handle censored demand in quick commerce?	Flag stock-out periods; exclude or impute them; model sales as min(demand, stock); use availability as a feature; treat forecasts as unconstrained demand.	chapter ch::45
[Ch 45] How do you make forecasts at store level add up to city level?	Hierarchical reconciliation: bottom-up, top-down, or optimal combination (MinT), and evaluate at the levels where decisions are made.	chapter ch::45
[Ch 45] Decompose ETA or predict end to end?	Components help dispatch and diagnosis and allow targeted fixes; end-to-end often gives the best customer-facing number; combine by feeding component predictions into an end-to-end model.	chapter ch::45
[Ch 45] Your P90 forecast covers 80% of outcomes. What do you do?	Recalibrate with conformal prediction or an empirical correction on recent residuals, check by segment and horizon, and investigate drift (new demand patterns, events).	chapter ch::45
[Ch 45] How would you rebalance couriers?	Forecast need per zone for the next window, solve a min-cost transport LP from surplus to deficit zones, then translate moves into incentives or suggestions; evaluate with a switchback test.	chapter ch::45
[Ch 45] Design ETA prediction for a new city with little data. [Ch 45] A new dispatch algorithm reduces average pickup time in simulation. How do you launch it? [Ch 45] How do forecast errors translate into business cost, and how would you choose the loss?	Under-forecast causes late deliveries and lost orders; over-forecast causes idle courier pay or wasted stock. Estimate both costs, and choose the quantile q = cost_under / (cost_under + cost_over) (the newsvendor result) as the forecast target.	chapter ch::45
[Ch 45] How would you decide delivery fees by zone and time?	Estimate elasticities with randomised experiments; model demand and supply responses; optimise fees for marketplace-level objectives (orders delivered on time, contribution, fairness) under constraints; test with switchbacks; watch long-term retention.	chapter ch::45
[Ch 46] Why not rank every item with your best model? (M)	Latency and cost. Scoring a million items with a deep model per request is far beyond a 150 ms budget. A cheap retrieval stage cuts to a few hundred candidates with high recall; the expensive model then only needs to be precise at the top.	chapter ch::46
[Ch 46] Your offline NDCG improved by 3%, but the A/B test is flat. Why? (M)	Common causes: the offline metric is computed on logged data that only contains items the old model showed, so it cannot judge new items; the offline label (click) differs from the online goal (order); a train/serve skew (a feature computed differently online); or the improvement sits in a segment too small to move the total. I would check feature parity first, then slice both results.	chapter ch::46
[Ch 46] How do you handle position bias in training data? (M)	Add the position as a feature during training and set it to a fixed value at serving time, so the model learns the item's quality separately from where it was shown. Alternatives: weight examples by the inverse of the estimated probability of being examined at that position, or collect unbiased data from a small randomised slice of traffic.	chapter ch::46
[Ch 46] How would you deal with cold start for a new restaurant? (M)	Use content features that exist from day one (cuisine, price, menu text embeddings, location), borrow signal from similar restaurants, and reserve a few exploration slots in the feed so the new restaurant collects interactions. A bandit can decide how much exposure to give as evidence comes in.	chapter ch::46
[Ch 46] Pointwise, pairwise or listwise loss: which and why? (S)	Pointwise gives calibrated probabilities, which I need if I combine scores (P(click) × P(order)) or apply thresholds. Pairwise and listwise optimise order directly and usually do better on NDCG. In practice I often start pointwise with gradient boosting, then try LambdaRank and compare on NDCG and on the A/B test.	chapter ch::46
[Ch 46] How do you know the model isn't creating a feedback loop? (S)	Track catalogue coverage and the concentration of impressions over time (for example the share going to the top 1% of restaurants). Keep a small exploration share of traffic, and evaluate new models partly on that randomised data, which is free of the loop.	chapter ch::46
[Ch 46] When would you not use ML at all? (S)	When a rule captures most of the value, when there is too little data to beat it, when the decision must be fully explainable, or when the cost of maintenance outweighs the gain. I'd ship the rule first, log the data, and revisit when it stops scaling.	chapter ch::46
[Ch 46] How would you choose between real-time and batch inference? (M)	By how quickly the input changes and how much freshness is worth. Recommendations that depend on the current session or real-time availability need online scoring; a daily email or a nightly taxonomy classification can be batch. A common hybrid: batch-computed candidates, re-ranked online with session features.	chapter ch::46
[Ch 46] How do you evaluate an LLM-based component before launch? (M)	Build a labelled eval set of a few hundred real cases, including hard ones; measure task metrics; for open-ended outputs, use an LLM judge that has been checked against human labels on a sample; then run an A/B test on the business metric. Error analysis by hand comes before and after.	chapter ch::46
[Ch 46] Your LLM pipeline costs too much. What do you do? (S)	Measure where tokens go first. Then, in rough order of effort: shorten prompts and outputs, cache repeated calls, use batch APIs, route easy cases to a smaller model, and finally distil into a small fine-tuned model trained on the large model's outputs. Re-check quality on the eval set at each step.	chapter ch::46
[Ch 46] A key feature is computed differently in training and serving. How would you catch it? (S)	Log the features actually used at serving time and compare their distributions with the training features (train/serve skew checks). Better still, compute features once in a feature store used by both paths, so the logic cannot diverge.	chapter ch::46
[Ch 46] What would you build first, with a team of two and three months? (S)	A baseline with good logging: a simple retrieval (popularity plus "ordered before"), a gradient-boosted ranker on solid features, an offline evaluation pipeline and an A/B framework. Deep models and LLM components come after the data and evaluation loop works, because without them I cannot tell whether anything better is actually better.	chapter ch::46
[Diagnostic 1] A fraud detector has 95% recall and a 1% false-positive rate. Fraud is 0.1% of transactions. What fraction of flagged transactions are fraud?	Bayes: true positives 0.001 × 0.95 = 0.00095; false positives 0.999 × 0.01 = 0.00999; precision 0.00095 / 0.01094 ≈ 8.7%. At low prevalence, precision is dominated by the false-positive rate; report precision at the real base rate, not on a balanced test set (Ch 4).	diagnostic
[Diagnostic 2] Training with cross-entropy minimises a KL divergence. Which one, in which direction, and what does that direction do to the model's behaviour? Where does the other direction appear in LLM training?	Cross-entropy on data = H(p_data) + KL(p_data ‖ p_model); the entropy is fixed, so training minimises forward KL. Forward KL is mode-covering: the model must put mass wherever the data does, including unlikely corners. Reverse KL (mode-seeking) appears as the KL penalty in RLHF and in on-policy distillation (Ch 1, 18).	diagnostic
[Diagnostic 3] A colleague checks an A/B test every day and stops when p < 0.05. What goes wrong, roughly how badly, and what should they do instead?	Repeated peeking inflates the false-positive rate well above 5%: an A/A test checked daily for 20 days reaches "significance" about 25% of the time. Fix: a pre-registered sample size from a power analysis and one look at the end, or a sequential method designed for peeking (alpha spending, always-valid p-values) (Ch 2).	diagnostic
[Diagnostic 4] On the same 500 test items, model A scores 82% and model B 84%. Is B better? How would you decide?	Standard error of each accuracy ≈ 1.7 points; an unpaired difference has SE ≈ 2.4 points, so 2 points is within noise. Because both models see the same items, use a paired test (McNemar, or a paired bootstrap over items), which depends only on items where they disagree and has lower variance. If still unclear, collect more items or look at slices (Ch 2, 23).	diagnostic
[Diagnostic 5] A model has 0.97 validation AUC and performs badly in production. List the causes you would check, in order.	In order: leakage (features from after the prediction time, target leakage, duplicates or the same users across splits, a random split where a time split was needed); training–serving skew (features computed differently online); a label definition that differs from the production outcome; distribution shift since the data was collected; and the metric itself (AUC fine, but the operating threshold or calibration wrong) (Ch 3, 47).	diagnostic
[Diagnostic 6] A 10-class classifier's loss at step 0 is 7.5. What should it be, and what does 7.5 suggest?	About ln 10 ≈ 2.30. A loss of 7.5 means the initial logits are far too large or confident: wrong output-layer initialisation, a missing or double softmax, labels misaligned or out of range. Fix the start before tuning anything (Ch 5).	diagnostic
[Diagnostic 7] Write the AdamW update. Why is its weight decay "decoupled", and why does that matter?	m ← β₁m + (1 − β₁)g; v ← β₂v + (1 − β₂)g²; bias-correct; θ ← θ − η(m̂/(√v̂ + ε) + λθ). In Adam with L2 the decay term passes through the adaptive scaling, so weights with large gradient variance are barely regularised; AdamW applies decay directly to the weights, so it acts uniformly and is tunable independently of the learning rate (Ch 7).	diagnostic
[Diagnostic 8] Why does Adam need learning-rate warm-up? What does a warmup–stable–decay (WSD) schedule give you that cosine does not?	Early second-moment estimates are poor and small, so the first steps are large and noisy; warm-up keeps them small until v is reliable. WSD holds the learning rate constant and decays only at the end, so any checkpoint in the stable phase can be decayed into a finished model: you can extend runs and branch experiments without retraining (Ch 7).	diagnostic
[Diagnostic 9] Why is bf16 preferred to fp16 for training, and why keep fp32 master weights anyway?	bf16 has fp32's exponent range, so gradients do not overflow or underflow and no loss scaling is needed; fp16 has more mantissa but a narrow range. bf16 has only 8 bits of precision, so small updates to weights near 1 round away; fp32 master weights accumulate them (Ch 7).	diagnostic
[Diagnostic 10] You double the width of a model under the standard parametrisation and keep the learning rate. What happens, and how does µP change it?	Under SP, each Adam step changes the output roughly in proportion to width, so the learning rate tuned at the smaller width becomes too large and training can diverge; every width needs its own sweep. µP scales initialisation and per-layer learning rates (hidden-matrix learning rates ∝ 1/width) so the best learning rate transfers across widths (Ch 7).	diagnostic
[Diagnostic 11] Estimate the KV cache for one 8,192-token sequence: 32 layers, 8 KV heads, head dimension 128, bf16. How do GQA and MLA change it?	2 (K and V) × 32 × 8 × 128 × 8,192 × 2 bytes ≈ 1.07 GB. With 32 full heads (MHA) it would be 4× larger; GQA shares KV heads across query heads; MLA caches a small latent per token instead of keys and values, a further several-fold cut (Ch 10, 11, 21).	diagnostic
[Diagnostic 12] Why is the attention score divided by √d_k?	With unit-variance components, q·k has variance d_k; unscaled scores grow with dimension, the softmax saturates towards one-hot and its gradients vanish. Dividing by √d_k keeps the variance near 1 (Ch 10).	diagnostic
[Diagnostic 13] How does RoPE encode position? Why does a model trained at 8k tokens degrade at 32k, and what are two fixes?	RoPE rotates pairs of query and key dimensions by angles proportional to position, so q·k depends only on relative distance. Beyond the training length, low-frequency dimensions reach rotation angles never seen in training. Fixes: position interpolation, NTK-aware scaling, YaRN, followed by a short fine-tune on long sequences (Ch 10).	diagnostic
[Diagnostic 14] You have 1e23 training FLOPs. Using Chinchilla's rule of thumb, what model size and token count are compute-optimal? Why do most open models train far smaller models on far more tokens?	C ≈ 6ND with D ≈ 20N gives C ≈ 120N², so N ≈ √(1e23/120) ≈ 29B parameters and D ≈ 580B tokens. [Diagnostic 15] A mixture-of-experts layer has 64 experts with top-2 routing. What scales with the number of experts and what with k? Why does it need load balancing, and how is it done?	Memory (parameters) scales with the number of experts; compute per token scales with k (2 experts' FFNs). [Diagnostic 18] How does MinHash deduplication find near-duplicate documents, and why does deduplication improve models?	Split each document into shingles (word n-grams), hash with many hash functions and keep the minimum per function; the share of matching minima estimates Jaccard similarity. Locality-sensitive hashing (bands) finds candidate pairs without comparing every pair. Duplicates waste compute, raise memorisation and distort the data mix (Ch 16).	diagnostic
[Diagnostic 19] Data, tensor and pipeline parallelism: what does each split, what does each communicate, and where in a cluster does each run?	Data parallelism replicates the model and splits the batch, all-reducing gradients (any network, across nodes). Tensor parallelism splits each layer's matrices, all-reducing activations in every layer (needs NVLink, inside a node). Pipeline parallelism splits the layers into stages and sends activations between them (tolerates slower links; costs a pipeline bubble). Large runs combine all three, often with ZeRO/FSDP sharding on the data-parallel axis (Ch 17).	diagnostic

[Diagnostic 21] Derive the policy gradient for J(θ) = E_(y∼π_θ)[R(y)]. Why does subtracting a baseline leave it unbiased?	∇J = Σ_y ∇π(y) R(y) = Σ_y π(y) ∇log π(y) R(y) = E[R(y) ∇log π(y)]. [Diagnostic 27] How does speculative decoding produce exactly the target model's distribution? When does it stop helping?
